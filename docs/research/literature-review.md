## 1. The claim, and what it has to concede

The thesis says that indexing systems flatten, that flattening hides, and that hiding which cannot be seen or
undone takes away a person's ability to judge for themselves. The literature supports this in a narrower form
than the plain statement "summaries are harmful", and the narrow form is the one worth defending.

The concession comes first. Simon (1971) observed that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of
attention": compression is a service to a reader with limited attention. Gigerenzer and colleagues (2007) show
that simplified formats for health statistics help patients decide. Porter argues that numbers let strangers
hold experts to account. The Air Quality Index itself was created because American cities were using many
different indices that confused the public (EPA, 1999). A thesis that opposed summarising would be arguing
against all of this. The thesis as written does not: its first sentence says flattening makes the world
usable.

## 2. Flattening: a term the thesis has to define

"Flattening" has no settled meaning in the scholarship. Chayka uses it for the sameness of culture under
recommendation systems. Steyerl (2011) uses it for the "mathematical, flattened, infinite, continuous, and
homogenous space" of linear perspective. Latour (1986) describes flat inscriptions that let someone master the
world from a distance. These are three different things.

A definition can be built from established terms. Espeland and Stevens (1998) call the comparison of unlike
things by a common measure *commensuration*, and note that it reduces information and turns qualities into
quantities. Latour (1999) calls the process by which working machinery disappears behind its own success
*black-boxing*, and argues that an abstraction stays legitimate while the chain of steps behind it can be
followed in both directions. Put together: flattening is commensuration with the path back removed.

This is also what the people who build indexes ask of themselves. The OECD and European Commission's handbook
on composite indicators (2008) defends them as tools for summarising, on the condition that they can be taken
apart again. So "a summary should open" is a professional norm that many public summaries fail to meet, and
the thesis can say so. Davis, Kingsbury and Merry (2012) add that an indicator carries a theory of what is
good, and moves power toward whoever produces it: the decision about what to hide is someone's decision.

Three sources complicate "opening". Drucker (2011) writes that "data are capta, taken not given", and Gitelman's
collection makes the same point: a summary opens into another made thing, not into raw truth. Ananny and
Crawford (2018) argue that seeing inside a system is not the same as understanding it or having power over it.
Star and Strauss show that making things visible can serve surveillance as well as recognition. The thesis's
word "undo" answers the first two: opening must lead to something the person can act on.

## 3. Whose number: populations and persons

The second half of the argument is that a summary made for everyone is presented to someone. Molenaar (2004)
gives the formal ground: what holds for variation between people holds for one person over time only under
conditions that human processes rarely meet. Sen's capability approach supplies the idea of an *informational
basis*, what an evaluation lets in and what it leaves out, and of the differences between people that turn the
same conditions into different outcomes. Bowker and Star (1999) describe the strain on a life read through a
category built for a population. Hacking (2006) describes how classifications change the people classified:
"The target has moved. I call this the 'looping effect'." Nguyen (2024) argues that a person's own values can be
replaced by a simplified measure supplied from outside.

Fourcade and Healy complicate this: platform scores are individual and still disempowering. A number being
"yours" is not enough; it also has to open, and to be yours to use.

## 4. Legibility, from both sides

Scott (1998) used legibility for the way a state makes people and land readable in order to administer them.
The author's essay "The Legible Web" uses the word the other way round: "whether a user can see and understand
what a web technology is doing, who controls it and what it does with their behavior". No source was found that
joins the two senses. They fit together if legibility is taken to have a direction: the same word names who
gets to read whom, and flattening buys legibility for the system at the cost of legibility for the person. Rao
(2010) is the usual bridge from Scott to technology. Scott's critics keep this honest: Li (2005) asks what a
scheme does and for whom, not only whether it fails. Scott's own claim is narrower than it is often quoted: the
harm comes from simplification imposed without recourse.

On the web itself, Lialina (2012) argues that the invisible computer also makes its user invisible, and in an
earlier essay (2005) writes: "The amateur web didn't die and it has not disappeared but it is hidden." Ranking
is the mechanism of hiding. Helmond names the shift platformization. Two measurements show the distance back
growing: 19% of mobile pages point to public source maps (Web Almanac, 2024), and people who saw an AI summary
in search results clicked through to a source about half as often as those who did not (Pew, 2025; the finding
is disputed by Google).

## 5. The index in question: air quality

The Air Quality Index is documented by its maker, which makes it an unusually clear case. The EPA's technical
document tells agencies to take the highest reading among an area's monitors, convert each pollutant to a
common scale, and report only the highest: "The AQI is the highest value calculated for each pollutant." Its
worked example reports 126 for a day when ozone is at 126, fine particles at 102 and carbon monoxide at 90. The
1999 rule states that a breakpoint "does not imply any sort of health-effects threshold". In 2024 the boundary
between Good and Moderate for fine particles was lowered, and the same air changed colour.

Index designers named the defect decades ago: taking a maximum does not register changes in the other
pollutants (Swamee and Tyagi, 1999; the term "eclipsing" is credited to Ott, 1978). Cromar and colleagues
(2020) found that the index tracked respiratory emergency visits in seasons dominated by one pollutant and not
in Southern California's warm season, when the mixture is complex. Canada's health index (Stieb et al., 2008)
combines pollutants, and is still one number.

The distance between the index and a person is also measured. A review of 18 studies put the median
correlation between outdoor monitor readings and personal exposure to fine particles at 0.54 (Avery et al.,
2010). Sensitivity to cold varies between individuals (Koskela, 2007), and a review of pollen found no
agreement on the levels at which symptoms begin. People's own lists of their triggers agree only modestly with
tests (Janssens and Ritz, 2013).

The evidence that the index works has to be stated too. Alerts reduce outdoor activity (Neidell, 2009), and
adults with asthma respond more than others (Mirabelli et al., 2020). But adherence to alert advice is low, and
many people act on their own sense of the air (D'Antoni et al., 2017). The fair summary is that the index works
as a broadcast and falls short as information for one person.

The nearest precedent is the Louisville study, which joined timestamped rescue-inhaler use to outdoor
conditions (Su et al., 2017). It worked at the level of the day and the city, and its authors note they had no
data on individual sensitivity. In the humanities, Gabrys, Pritchard and Barratt (2016) argue for "just good
enough data" that is honest about its coarseness; Calvillo asks, "What if instead of making data visible, we
make it sensible?"; Shapiro (2015) treats the body as an instrument. Two authors would question the project as a
whole. Kenner worries that tracking moves responsibility for polluted air onto the person breathing it, and
Liboiron warns against answering one threshold with another.

## 6. Other sealed summaries: models and feeds

Machine learning offers the most literal case of flattening. Methods that draw thousands of dimensions as a
flat map are described by their own practitioners as misleading to read: Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson (2016)
head their sections "Cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot mean nothing" and "Distances between clusters might not mean
anything". Chari and Pachter (2023) write that such reduction "inevitably induces significant distortion".
Burrell (2016) separates three kinds of opacity: secrecy, lack of skill, and the mismatch between what a model
computes and what a person can read. Crawford and Paglen (2019) describe training labels that "compress and
simplify images". For birds, Cornell's account of its own app says the sound is turned into an image and then
into a species name. No scholarly critique of bird identification apps was found.

The caution here is strong. Seaver (2017) argues that treating algorithms as black boxes helps make them so.
Kaur and colleagues (2020) found that data scientists over-trust interpretability tools, and Bansal and
colleagues found that explanations raised acceptance of a system's advice whether or not it was right. The
constructive vocabulary is contestability (Kluttz, Kohli and Mulligan) and seams (Ehsan et al., 2024): the
person can trace and dispute, which is more than being shown.

For feeds, Monge Roffarello, Lukoff and De Russis (2023) list infinite scroll among eleven "attention capture
damaging patterns". Lukoff and colleagues (2021) and Baughan and colleagues (2022) measure a reduced sense of
agency while scrolling. The word "addiction" is contested (Panova and Carbonell, 2018; Orben and Przybylski,
2019), so the firmer claim is a designed loss of agency. Grosser (2014) is the nearest precedent for an artwork
paired with an essay: "The primary thing that Facebook metrics want is increased user engagement with the
site."

## 7. Designing summaries that open

Human-computer interaction has long had an answer to "summary first": Shneiderman's (1996) overview, then
detail on demand. Bellotti and Edwards (2001) ask that a system make clear what it knows and how. Dörk and
colleagues (2013) give four principles for critical visualisation: disclosure, plurality, contingency,
empowerment. Chalmers and Galani argue for showing the seams of a system.

On showing uncertainty, Hullman examines why authors leave it out; Boukhelifa and colleagues (2012) found
people preferred dashed lines as a cue for uncertain data; Song and Szafir (2019) studied how to mark missing
values, and found that visible gaps lower trust in the data, which here is the intended effect. Gaver, Beaver
and Benford (2003) treat ambiguity as "a resource for design".

On personal data, the evidence divides. Ancker and colleagues (2015) found that people with several chronic
conditions experienced tracking as a reminder of illness; Etkin (2016) found that measuring an activity can
reduce enjoyment of it. Against this, Harkin and colleagues (2016) found that monitoring progress helps people
reach goals; verbal terms for likelihood are read very inconsistently (Budescu et al., 2009; Knapp et al.,
2004); and a correlation described in text is the form most likely to be read as a cause (Xiong et al., 2020).
A further caution: most use of a personal tracker is a glance of a few seconds (Gouveia et al., 2016).
Reviewing 161 papers, Bennett and colleagues (2023) found that HCI's own use of "agency" remains ambiguous,
which is why thesis.md defines it as something a person can be seen to do.

On capture, the support is firmer: recording in the moment beats recall (Shiffman et al., 2008), and partly
automated tracking lowers the burden (Choe et al., 2017). Jones and Kelly (2018) found that filtering the
patterns shown to people made the remainder more useful. Rudin argues for models that are interpretable by
construction, in place of explanations added afterwards.
