## 1. Construction: how the US AQI is computed, what the maximum operator hides, PSI 1976 → AQI 1999, colours, the 2024 PM2.5 update

### Takeaway
The AQI is a chain of deliberate reductions: each pollutant's concentration (already an 8-hour, 24-hour or 1-hour statistic) is taken at the *highest monitor* in a reporting area, mapped by piecewise-linear interpolation onto a 0–500 scale pinned so that 100 ≈ the national standard, and then only the *highest* pollutant sub-index is reported, with a colour. EPA's own documents say simplicity and a uniform message were the design goals; the cost is that co-exposure, the identity and count of other elevated pollutants, monitor distance, and averaging time all drop out of the number a person sees.

### Cited Findings

**Primary documents**

- **Regulatory basis.** US EPA, "Air Quality Index Reporting; Final Rule," 64 FR 42530, 4 August 1999 (40 CFR Part 58, §58.50 and Appendix G), effective 4 October 1999. — [Federal Register / govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm). The codified text is 40 CFR Part 58 Appendix G, "Uniform Air Quality Index (AQI) and Daily Reporting" — [eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-58/appendix-Appendix%20G%20to%20Part%2058) (the eCFR page blocked automated access this session; the Appendix G text was read in the 1999 Federal Register version and as reproduced in the EPA technical assistance document below).
- **Technical assistance document.** US EPA, Office of Air Quality Planning and Standards, *Technical Assistance Document for the Reporting of Daily Air Quality – the Air Quality Index (AQI)*, EPA-403/B-26-003, May 2026 (the version currently served by AirNow; the full PDF text was extracted and read). — [EPA/AirNow PDF](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **2024 update.** US EPA, "Final Updates to the Air Quality Index (AQI) for Particulate Matter: Fact Sheet and Common Questions," February 2024 — [EPA PDF](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-02/pm-naaqs-air-quality-index-fact-sheet.pdf); final rule "Reconsideration of the National Ambient Air Quality Standards for Particulate Matter," published 6 March 2024 — [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/03/06/2024-02637/reconsideration-of-the-national-ambient-air-quality-standards-for-particulate-matter)

**The computation**

- Step 1 is a spatial maximum: "Identify the highest concentration among all of the monitors within each reporting area," then truncate (ozone to 3 decimals in ppm, PM2.5 to 1 decimal in µg/m³, PM10/SO2/NO2 to integers, CO to 1 decimal). — [EPA TAD 2026, §IV](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- Step 2–4 is piecewise-linear interpolation between two breakpoints (Equation 1): I_p = [(I_Hi − I_Lo)/(BP_Hi − BP_Lo)] × (C_p − BP_Lo) + I_Lo, rounded to the nearest integer. — [EPA TAD 2026, §IV](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- Step 5 is the pollutant maximum: "The AQI is the highest value calculated for each pollutant." The 1999 rule words it as the AQI being "equal to the highest of the numbers corresponding to each pollutant," and calls the responsible pollutant the "critical" pollutant (the 2026 TAD calls it the "main pollutant"). — [EPA TAD 2026](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf); [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- **EPA's own worked example shows the eclipse.** With 8-hour O3 = 0.078 ppm, PM2.5 = 35.9 µg/m³ and CO = 8.4 ppm, the sub-indices are 126, 102 and 90, and "The AQI is 126, with O3 as the main pollutant." A day with *two* pollutants above 100 (both "unhealthy for sensitive groups") and a third at 90 is reported identically to a day with ozone alone at 126. — [EPA TAD 2026, "How to handle values from multiple pollutants"](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **Averaging periods baked into each sub-index (Table 6 of the TAD):** O3 8-hour (and 1-hour above 0.125 ppm); PM2.5 24-hour; PM10 24-hour; CO 8-hour; SO2 1-hour (24-hour above 305 ppb); NO2 1-hour. — [EPA TAD 2026, Table 6](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **Current breakpoints (Table 6), by AQI band 0–50 / 51–100 / 101–150 / 151–200 / 201–300 / 301+:** PM2.5 24-h (µg/m³) 0.0–9.0 / 9.1–35.4 / 35.5–55.4 / 55.5–125.4 / 125.5–225.4 / 225.5+; O3 8-h (ppm) 0.000–0.054 / 0.055–0.070 / 0.071–0.085 / 0.086–0.105 / 0.106–0.200 / (1-hour values above); PM10 24-h (µg/m³) 0–54 / 55–154 / 155–254 / 255–354 / 355–424 / 425+; CO 8-h (ppm) 0.0–4.4 / 4.5–9.4 / 9.5–12.4 / 12.5–15.4 / 15.5–30.4 / 30.5+; SO2 1-h (ppb) 0–35 / 36–75 / 76–185 / 186–304 / 305–604 (24-h) / 605+; NO2 1-h (ppb) 0–53 / 54–100 / 101–360 / 361–649 / 650–1249 / 1250+. AQI 500 for PM2.5 corresponds to 325.4 µg/m³. — [EPA TAD 2026, Table 6](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **The official AQI is yesterday's.** "It takes a full 24 hours to obtain an AQI value... so you are in effect required to report yesterday's AQI." — [EPA TAD 2026, §II](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **NowCast** is EPA's bridge from hourly data to an index built on 24-hour/8-hour statistics. For PM it is a weighted average of the previous 12 hours: when air is stable the hours are weighted more evenly, and when it is changing fast recent hours dominate (approaching a 3-hour average); the weight comes from (max − min)/max over the 12 hours; 2 of the last 3 hours must be valid. PM2.5 method updated 1 August 2013, PM10 9 December 2014, ozone NowCast 1 August 2019. — [EPA TAD 2026, "How the NowCast is calculated"](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- EPA says plainly that hourly data cannot make an AQI: "It is not valid to use shorter-term (e.g., hourly) data to calculate an AQI value"; and on sensors, "EPA does not advise applying AQI values to realtime sensor data, especially 1-minute readings. Health studies do not tell us what a single minute of exposure to a pollutant may mean." — [EPA TAD 2026, FAQ](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **Who must report:** Metropolitan Statistical Areas with population over 350,000; "Towns and cities with 350,000 or fewer inhabitants are not required to report the AQI." Required content: reporting area, period, main pollutant, AQI value, category and colour, and "sensitive groups for all pollutants with an AQI over 100." Pollutant concentrations, sub-area AQIs and other pollutants' values are *voluntary*. — [EPA TAD 2026, §II](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- EPA admits the spatial flattening in its own FAQ: when it looks smoky but the dial is green or yellow, "particle pollution levels can vary considerably, even over small distances and short periods of time," and "monitors in the same area may show different NowCast AQI categories." — [EPA TAD 2026, FAQ](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)

**History and the reasons for the colours**

- EPA established the Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) in 1976 (41 FR 37660) for voluntary use by state and local agencies, after an EPA / Council on Environmental Quality study found that 55 urban areas reporting an index used "14 different indices"; EPA said the diversity "sent a confusing message about air quality to the public." — [64 FR 42530, preamble](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- The 1999 rule renamed PSI the Air Quality Index (focus-group participants felt the name "more clearly identified the index as relating to the quality of the air"), added the "unhealthy for sensitive groups" category (101–150), added sub-indices for 8-hour ozone and PM2.5, and moved the reporting threshold from urbanised areas over 200,000 to MSAs over 350,000. — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- Categories and colours fixed in 1999: Good 0–50 green; Moderate 51–100 yellow; Unhealthy for sensitive groups 101–150 orange; Unhealthy 151–200 red; Very unhealthy 201–300 purple; Hazardous 301–500 maroon. Colours became *required* whenever the index is shown in colour because, without a rule, different agencies' colours "could well send a confusing message." — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- Why orange: some commenters wanted red for 101–150; EPA answered that red sends "too strong a message," while orange "sends an appropriate health message and yet a strong message that the standard has been exceeded." Only one new category was added to "maintain the degree of simplicity strongly supported by State and local agencies." — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- The index is normalised to regulation, not to bodies: "In most cases, the index value of 100 is associated with the numerical level of the short-term standard." For PM2.5 in 1999 the 100 point was set at 40 µg/m³, the midpoint between the annual (15) and 24-hour (65) standards, and the preamble says this "does not imply any sort of health-effects threshold." — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- In 1999 naming other pollutants was only encouraged: reports may include "The name and index value for other pollutants, particularly those with an index value greater than 100." — [64 FR 42530, Appendix G](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- The current TAD keeps the standard colours mandatory but now allows "ColorVision Assist" colours, developed with the South Coast air district, because the standard scale "can be difficult to discern" for people who have trouble telling red from green. — [EPA TAD 2026, Tables 2–3](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)

**The 2024 PM2.5 update (announced 7 February 2024, effective 6 May 2024)**

- Good/Moderate breakpoint lowered from 12.0 to 9.0 µg/m³ to match the new annual standard; 35.4 and 55.4 unchanged (24-hour standard retained at 35); Unhealthy upper bound lowered from 150.4 to 125.4; Very Unhealthy from 250.4 to 225.4; Hazardous now starts at 225.5 and the two former hazardous segments (250.5–350.4 and 350.5–500) were merged. — [EPA fact sheet, Feb 2024](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-02/pm-naaqs-air-quality-index-fact-sheet.pdf)
- EPA's own FAQ answer to "Did EPA update the AQI because my air quality is getting worse?" is "No": the same air is now a different colour. "Many areas can expect to see more days in the Moderate (Code Yellow) category," and wildfire days may shift from Unhealthy to Very Unhealthy or Very Unhealthy to Hazardous. — [EPA fact sheet, Feb 2024](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-02/pm-naaqs-air-quality-index-fact-sheet.pdf)
- Daily reporting is now defined as seven days a week on days with measured data, and submitting hourly data to AirNow satisfies the requirement. — [EPA TAD 2026, §II](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)

### Inferences
- The AQI contains **two maximum operators**, not one: the highest monitor in the area, then the highest pollutant. Both discard information a person with asthma could use (where the high reading was; what else was high).
- What the single number hides, itemised from the documents above: (a) co-exposure — the TAD example reports 126 whether or not PM2.5 is also over 100; (b) which pollutant, unless the "main pollutant" label is shown, and never the second or third; (c) monitor distance and which monitor — the reporting area is a metropolitan area and the value comes from its worst monitor; (d) averaging time — a 24-hour PM2.5 statistic and a 1-hour NO2 maximum sit on the same 0–500 scale; (e) age of the data — the official AQI is yesterday's, NowCast is a 12-hour weighted estimate; (f) that the scale is anchored to the legal standard, so the colour of a given concentration changes when the standard does (9.1–12.0 µg/m³ was green until 5 May 2024 and yellow after); (g) everything that is not a criteria pollutant: pollen, temperature, humidity, indoor air.
- "Green" means "below the standard for the single worst criteria pollutant at the worst monitor in the metro area over the averaging period." It does not mean "nothing here affects you." EPA itself said in 1999 that a breakpoint "does not imply any sort of health-effects threshold." This is the strongest primary-source support for showing a personal threshold beside the population one.
- The thesis project's choices map directly onto documented omissions: decomposing into pollutants reverses the pollutant maximum; showing distance and spatial scale reverses the spatial maximum; showing `asOf` exposes the averaging/lag; adding pollen and weather restores what the index was never built to include.
- The colours were chosen for *consistency across agencies* and to signal *exceedance of a standard* (orange), with comprehension tested in focus groups for the name and the "sensitive groups" wording. The Federal Register text read this session does not record a perceptual or colour-science rationale.

## 2. Critique in the scientific literature: eclipsing and ambiguity, multi-pollutant alternatives, comparative reviews, public (mis)reading, monitor sparsity and low-cost sensors

### Takeaway
The limits of a maximum-operator index are a named, formal property ("eclipsing") in the index-design literature, and health researchers have shown empirically that the AQI can fail to track respiratory risk when the mixture is complex. Canada and Hong Kong replaced this design with additive, no-threshold health indices. Separately, about half of US adults are aware of air quality alerts and a minority act on them, and most US counties have no PM2.5 monitor at all.

### Cited Findings

**Formal properties of aggregated indices**

- Ott, Wayne R. (1978). *Environmental Indices: Theory and Practice.* Ann Arbor, MI: Ann Arbor Science. xi + 371 pp. Covers the structure and design principles of environmental index systems, with chapters on air and water indices and a framework for comparing aggregation forms. Verified as a catalogue/OSTI record only; the text was not read. — [OSTI record](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/6681348-environmental-indices-theory-practice); [Texas A&M catalogue](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000264648). *Bearing:* the canonical source for treating "what an index hides" as a property of its mathematics.
- Swamee, P. K., & Tyagi, A. (1999). "Formation of an Air Pollution Index." *Journal of the Air & Waste Management Association* 49(1), 88–91. Abstract (as indexed): ambiguous aggregations cause needless alarm about cleaner air; eclipsed ones give false reassurance by rating heavily polluted air as less polluted; linear-sum and root-sum-square forms suffer from ambiguity; and "the maximum operator does not account for changes in the remaining pollutants." They propose an aggregation function free of both. Verified from the indexed abstract. — [journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/47484895). *Bearing:* gives the thesis precise vocabulary. The US AQI avoids ambiguity (it never cries wolf from many small values) by accepting insensitivity to everything but the worst pollutant.

**Multi-pollutant, health-based alternatives**

- Stieb, D. M., Burnett, R. T., Smith-Doiron, M., Brion, O., Shin, H. H., & Economou, V. (2008). "A new multipollutant, no-threshold air quality health index based on short-term associations observed in daily time-series analyses." *JAWMA* 58(3), 435–450. doi:10.3155/1047-3289.58.3.435. The abstract states that existing indices had been criticised for not capturing additive effects of multiple pollutants and for not reflecting the apparent no-threshold concentration–response relationship. The AQHI is the *sum* of excess mortality risk associated with individual pollutants from Canadian time-series studies, scaled 0–10, computed hourly from trailing 3-hour averages. Abstract-level verification. — [EPA HERO record](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/620207); [publisher page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.3155/1047-3289.58.3.435). *Bearing:* a government index that deliberately un-flattens along the pollutant axis and drops the idea of a safe threshold, while still being one number.
- Wong, T. W., et al. (2013). "Developing a risk-based air quality health index." *Atmospheric Environment* 76, 52– (pages as cited in a secondary reference list). A multi-pollutant AQHI for Hong Kong on the Canadian model using SO2, NO2, O3 and PM10; the Hong Kong government proposed replacing its Air Pollution Index with it. — [HKUST research portal](https://researchportal.hkust.edu.hk/en/publications/developing-a-risk-based-air-quality-health-index/); [Hong Kong Legislative Council paper, 2013](https://www.legco.gov.hk/yr12-13/english/panels/ea/papers/eacb1-352-1-e.pdf)
- Perlmutt, L. D., & Cromar, K. R. (2019). "Comparing associations of respiratory risk for the EPA Air Quality Index and health-based air quality indices." *Atmospheric Environment* (ScienceDirect PII S1352231019300330; volume and DOI not confirmed this session). Using the Bronx and Queens, a multi-pollutant index (NO2, O3, PM2.5, SO2 coefficients from 2005–2010) was tested against the EPA AQI on respiratory emergency visits in 2011–2013. The multi-pollutant index tracked visits more consistently across seasons; the EPA AQI showed no significant association in the high-ozone season. Conclusion: "a single-pollutant index may at times inadequately communicate the full adverse health risks of air pollution." — [NYU Marron Institute paper page](https://marroninstitute.nyu.edu/papers/comparing-associations-of-respiratory-risk-for-the-epa-air-quality-index-andhealth-based-air-quality-indices)
- Cromar, K. R., Ghazipura, M., Gladson, L. A., & Perlmutt, L. (2020). "Evaluating the U.S. Air Quality Index as a risk communication tool: Comparing associations of index values with respiratory morbidity among adults in California." *PLoS ONE* 15(11): e0242031. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0242031. 1,373,165 adult respiratory emergency visits, 25 counties, 2012–2014. Cool season (PM2.5-dominated; AQI–PM2.5 R² ≥ 0.89): AQI associated with visits in all three regions (pooled RR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04 per IQR). Warm season: no significant AQI association in Southern California even though individual pollutants were associated; in South Coast counties the pooled AQI estimate was 1.00 (0.98–1.01) while a health-based index was associated in both seasons (RR 1.02). The authors conclude "AQI values may not fully capture population-level respiratory health risks" when mixtures are complex, and note that monitors may not reflect population exposure. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7671501/). *Bearing:* this is also half of the counter-argument: the AQI works when one pollutant dominates.

**Reviews comparing indices worldwide**

- Plaia, A., & Ruggieri, M. (2011). "Air quality indices: a review." *Reviews in Environmental Science and Bio/Technology* 10(2), 165–179. doi:10.1007/s11157-010-9227-2. Groups the indices proposed in the literature or adopted by countries into categories and concludes there is no common strategy that allows comparison across cities under different directives. Verified from repository records; full text not read. — [University of Palermo repository](https://iris.unipa.it/handle/10447/62314); open working-paper version at [University of Bergamo](https://aisberg.unibg.it/handle/10446/949)
- Kanchan, Gorai, A. K., & Goyal, P. (2015). "A Review on Air Quality Indexing System." *Asian Journal of Atmospheric Environment* 9(2), 101–113. doi:10.5572/ajae.2015.9.2.101. Reviews the major indices worldwide and notes each method has characteristic strengths and weaknesses. Record-level verification. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/3cc0b87644ce4ffaa3ce1b636745d48d)

**Public awareness, understanding and behaviour**

- Mirabelli, M. C., Ebelt, S., & Damon, S. A. (2020). "Air Quality Index and air quality awareness among adults in the United States." *Environmental Research* 183, 109185. doi:10.1016/j.envres.2020.109185. 12,396 respondents, 2016–2018 ConsumerStyles surveys linked to county AQI. 53.9% were aware of air quality alerts; 29.2% thought or were told air quality was bad in the past year; 14.7% changed behaviour. Even in counties with 15+ days of AQI ≥ 101, "just 23% changed their behavior." Adults with asthma vs without: aware 58.7% vs 53.5%; changed behaviour 24.6% vs 14.0%. The paper also cites its authors' 2018 finding that 3% of adults had discussed reducing exposure with a health professional. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182097/)
- D'Antoni, D., Smith, L., Auyeung, V., & Weinman, J. (2017). "Psychosocial and demographic predictors of adherence and non-adherence to health advice accompanying air quality warning systems: a systematic review." *Environmental Health* 16, 100. doi:10.1186/s12940-017-0307-4. 21 studies. Actual adherence to advice to reduce or reschedule outdoor activity ranged from 9.7% to 57% (median 31%). Barriers included "lack of understanding of the indices," confusion with other indices (e.g. hot weather), low self-efficacy, and not knowing where to check. In four of six studies with self-reported reasons, many people acted on *their own perception* of bad air rather than the alert; in one study 31.1% of people with asthma changed behaviour because of media alerts alone, 25.6% because of perception alone. Facilitators included believing one's symptoms were due to air pollution and advice from a health professional. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5610416/). *Bearing:* people already use their bodies as instruments; a diary that formalises that is working with observed behaviour, not against it.

**Monitor sparsity, spatial representativeness, low-cost sensors**

- Sullivan, D. M., & Krupnick, A. (2018). "Using Satellite Data to Fill the Gaps in the US Air Pollution Monitoring Network." Resources for the Future working paper. "79 percent of US counties do not have a PM2.5 monitor"; satellite-derived estimates put 47.6 million people in areas failing the PM2.5 standard versus 23.3 million counted from monitors; 54 counties were wrongly classed as attaining. Verified from RFF's press release, not the paper. — [RFF press release](https://www.rff.org/news/press-releases/new-satellite-data-show-twice-as-many-americans-live-in-counties-not-meeting-fine-particulate-air-quality-standards-than-previously-thought/)
- NRDC analysis (Vijay Limaye): of 190 counties modelled at 8–12 µg/m³ PM2.5 in 2018, 118 (62%) had no PM2.5 monitor. Search-result summary only; an advocacy source. — [NRDC](https://www.nrdc.org/bio/vijay-limaye/local-air-quality-monitoring-lagging-across-us-nrdc-finds)
- Barkjohn, K. K., Gantt, B., & Clements, A. L. (2021). "Development and application of a United States-wide correction for PM2.5 data collected with the PurpleAir sensor." *Atmospheric Measurement Techniques* 14, 4617–4637. doi:10.5194/amt-14-4617-2021. 50 sensors at 39 sites in 16 states, about 12,000 collocated 24-hour pairs. Raw PurpleAir data "overestimate PM2.5 concentrations by about 40% in most parts of the U.S." Correction: PM2.5 = 0.524 × PA_cf1 − 0.0862 × RH + 5.75; adding humidity cut RMSE from 8 to 3 µg/m³. Applied on the AirNow Fire and Smoke Map and offered on PurpleAir as the "US EPA" option. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8422884/); [journal page](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/14/4617/2021/)
- In the Louisville inhaler-sensor study (section 3), ozone "data were only available from three locations throughout Jefferson County at 8-hr intervals," and pollen and mould came from one rooftop monitor, so every inhaler event that day got the same pollen count. — [Su et al. 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5289907/)

### Inferences
- "Eclipsing" (Ott; Swamee & Tyagi) is the exact technical name for what the thesis calls flattening on the pollutant axis. Using the term shows the argument is not only a humanities reading: index designers named the defect in 1978.
- The AQHI literature shows the alternative to a flattening index is usually *another* index. Canada un-flattens pollutants but still issues one population number. The thesis project differs in kind: it keeps components separate and adds the person's own record as the comparison.
- The PurpleAir story is a good parallel for the app's "honest numbers" rule: a dense network trades accuracy for proximity, and the correction factor plus the humidity term is exactly the kind of provenance that an index colour hides.
- A candid caveat for the defence: the AQI is not shown to be *wrong* at the population level when one pollutant dominates (Cromar et al. 2020, cool season). The critique is strongest for mixed-pollutant seasons, for places far from monitors, and for individuals.

- No source read this session directly measures *misreading* of the AQI number (e.g. taking it for a concentration or a percentage). D'Antoni et al. report "lack of understanding of the indices" without numbers. **Unverified leads:** Johnson, B. B. (2012), "Experience with urban air pollution in Paterson, New Jersey and implications for air pollution communication," *Risk Analysis* 32(1); Ramírez, A. S., et al. (2019) on environmental health literacy and air pollution in *Journal of Health Communication* 24(1); Borbet, T. C., et al. (2018) on AQI awareness and use in Mexico City, *BMC Public Health* 18.

- Not verified: Ott's own definitions of "eclipsing" and "ambiguity" (book not read); Perlmutt & Cromar 2019 volume/pages/DOI; Wong et al. 2013 full author list and pages.

## 3. Individual versus population: heterogeneity of response, personal versus ambient exposure, personalised tools

### Takeaway
Three bodies of evidence support a personal threshold beside the population one: controlled-exposure work shows people differ widely and *reproducibly* in their response to the same ozone dose; exposure science shows a central monitor explains only part of what a given person breathes; and inhaler-sensor studies show that passively logged rescue-inhaler use can be tied to conditions, though so far mostly at the group level and with coarse environmental data.

### Cited Findings

**Heterogeneity of response**

- McDonnell's 1989 review (ASTM symposium paper) states that individuals differ widely in the magnitude of their acute respiratory responses to ozone, and that these differences are reproducible, pointing to intrinsic responsiveness. Seen as a search-result summary only. — [ASTM record](https://store.astm.org/stp22828s.html)
- "Ozone-induced airway inflammatory changes differ between individuals and are reproducible" (American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, 1999; PubMed ID 10051250; authors not confirmed this session, I believe Holz et al.). In healthy and asthmatic subjects exposed repeatedly to 250 ppb ozone, the FEV1 fall was reproducible within individuals (r = 0.77, p < 0.001, as summarised in search results). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10051250)
- EPA's own framing concedes the point at category level: the AQI's "unhealthy for sensitive groups" band exists because sensitive groups differ by pollutant, and the 1999 rule required PM2.5 and PM10 to be reported separately because they have different sensitive groups and cautionary statements. — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)

**Personal exposure versus ambient monitors**

- Avery, C. L., Mills, K. T., Williams, R., McGraw, K. A., Poole, C., Smith, R. L., & Whitsel, E. A. (2010). "Estimating Error in Using Ambient PM2.5 Concentrations as Proxies for Personal Exposures: A Review." *Epidemiology* 21(2), 215–223. 18 studies, 619 non-smoking participants aged 6–93 in 17 European and North American cities: median within-participant ambient–personal correlation 0.54 (range 0.09–0.83). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/20075021); [UNC record](https://cpc.unc.edu/resources/publications/bib/9605)
- Avery et al. (2010). "Estimating Error in Using Residential Outdoor PM2.5 Concentrations as Proxies for Personal Exposures: A Meta-Analysis." *Environmental Health Perspectives* 118(5), 673–678. Similar result for a monitor right outside the home. Record-level verification. — [UNC record](https://www.cpc.unc.edu/resources/publications/bib/9606/)

**Personalised / sensor-based asthma studies**

- Su, J. G., Barrett, M. A., Henderson, K., Humblet, O., Smith, T., Sublett, J. W., Nesbitt, L., Hogg, C., Van Sickle, D., & Sublett, J. L. (2017). "Feasibility of Deploying Inhaler Sensors to Identify the Impacts of Environmental Triggers and Built Environment Factors on Asthma Short-Acting Bronchodilator Use." *Environmental Health Perspectives* 125(2), 254–261. doi:10.1289/EHP266. 140 participants in Louisville, 5,660 rescue-inhaler events, 13 June 2012 – 28 February 2014 (Propeller Health sensors). Per IQR increase, adjusted for weather: AQI rate ratio 1.055 (95% CI 1.003–1.108), PM10 1.054 (1.002–1.109); weed pollen and mould positive; tree cover protective (0.825). Ozone results were inconsistent. Limits the authors state: only 23% of events had GPS location; the analysis unit was the day, not the person; they "did not have" data on asthma severity or sensitivity to triggers; "we were limited to addressing outdoor exposures only"; the app collected self-reported triggers but the paper did not analyse them. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5289907/)
- Barrett, M., Combs, V., Su, J. G., Henderson, K., Tuffli, M., & the AIR Louisville Collaborative (2018). "AIR Louisville: Addressing Asthma With Technology, Crowdsourcing, Cross-Sector Collaboration, And Policy." *Health Affairs* 37(4), 525–534. doi:10.1377/hlthaff.2017.1315 (author list and pages from memory; title, journal and DOI stem confirmed). Reported a 78% reduction in rescue inhaler use and 48% improvement in symptom-free days among participants (about 1,147 enrolled per press coverage), and used the pooled data for city recommendations on tree canopy and zoning. Numbers seen in press coverage and search summaries, not the article; the study had no control group as far as I could see, and Propeller Health employees were authors. — [Health Affairs PDF](https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/pdf/10.1377/hlthaff.2017.1315)

### Inferences
- The Louisville study is the closest precedent for the thesis project and also its foil. It proves the mechanism (a timestamped, located puff joined to conditions) but answers a *population* question (which triggers matter in Louisville) and feeds city policy. The individual gets the app; the pattern goes to the city. The thesis design returns the pattern to the person who generated it.
- The Louisville limitations read like a specification for the thesis app's provenance fields: one pollen monitor for a whole city, three ozone sites, outdoor-only data, most events without location. Showing `distanceKm` and `spatialScale` is the honest response to exactly these conditions.
- An ambient–personal correlation with a median of 0.54 and a range from 0.09 to 0.83 means the monitor is a reasonable proxy for some people and nearly useless for others. That is a quantitative basis for labelling values "outdoor" and "regional" rather than "your air."
- A caution to carry into the defence: a personal threshold learned from a few dozen logs is itself a flattening and is statistically fragile. The app's gate (minimum counts, comparison with baseline hours, "looks more like your inhaler days" rather than a risk percentage) is the design answer; it should be presented as such.

### Gaps
- **Cold air:** no source verified this session for individual variation in the temperature at which cold triggers symptoms, nor for a population "cold" threshold such as freezing. **Unverified lead:** Koskela, H. O. (2007), "Cold air-provoked respiratory symptoms: the mechanisms and management," *International Journal of Circumpolar Health* 66(2), 91–100. The app's example sentence ("for most people cold starts at freezing") needs its own citation or softer wording.
- **Pollen:** the Springer page was paywalled to the fetch tool. **Unverified lead:** Steckling-Muschack, N., et al. (2021), "A systematic review of threshold values of pollen concentrations for symptoms of allergy," *Aerobiologia* 37, 395–424, doi:10.1007/s10453-021-09709-4; I recall it finding widely varying thresholds across studies and regions, but this was not confirmed.
- Ozone responder literature: only abstract-level confirmation. McDonnell's dose–response models and the EPA Integrated Science Assessment for Ozone (2020) would give citable figures on inter-individual variability; not retrieved.
- No study was found of an app that shows a *personal* threshold beside a population one, or of whether doing so changes behaviour or sense of agency. I found no evidence either way; this appears to be an open design-research question.
- "Precision environmental health" as a named programme (e.g. NIEHS strategic framing; Baccarelli et al.) was not searched.

## 4. STS and environmental humanities on sensing air and citizen sensing

### Takeaway
This literature supplies the thesis's theoretical spine: standards and thresholds are political instruments that decide whose air counts (Ottinger, Liboiron, Murphy); monitoring is a made thing with its own errors and judgement calls (Garnett, Calvillo); bodies are legitimate sensing instruments (Shapiro, Kenner, Calvillo); and data that is "just good enough" for a purpose can be more useful to the people affected than regulatory-grade data they cannot act on (Gabrys).

### Cited Findings

- **Gabrys, Jennifer; Pritchard, Helen; Barratt, Benjamin (2016).** "Just good enough data: Figuring data citizenships through air pollution sensing and data stories." *Big Data & Society* 3(2), 1–14. doi:10.1177/2053951716679677. Open access. From participatory air sensing with residents near fracking infrastructure in north-eastern Pennsylvania, argues that citizen data need not meet regulatory standards to be useful: it can be "just good enough" to indicate a problem and open questions, and "data stories" compose readings with context and observation. — [Goldsmiths repository](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/19511). *Bearing:* licenses a diary whose numbers are labelled regional and coarse yet still support a personal pattern; and "data stories" is a precedent for the app's one-sentence narrator sitting on top of counts.
- **Gabrys, Jennifer (2016).** *Program Earth: Environmental Sensing Technology and the Making of a Computational Planet.* University of Minnesota Press (Electronic Mediations). Sensors do not just record environments; they "generate new environments and environmental relations" and shape the kinds of citizens and collectives people become. Three parts: wild sensing, pollution sensing, urban sensing. — [University of Minnesota Press](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816693146/program-earth/). *Bearing:* an index is not a neutral window; it produces a kind of citizen (one who checks a colour). The app proposes a different one (one who keeps a record).
- **Gabrys, Jennifer (2019).** *How to Do Things with Sensors.* University of Minnesota Press, Forerunners: Ideas First no. 34. On the how-to as a prevailing digital genre and how citizen-sensing instructions make worlds "sense-able and actionable." Year taken from the Manifold posting date (August 2019). — [Manifold edition](https://manifold.umn.edu/projects/how-to-do-things-with-sensors)
- **Gabrys, Jennifer (2022).** *Citizens of Worlds: Open-Air Toolkits for Environmental Struggle.* University of Minnesota Press. Asks who counts as a "citizen" in citizen sensing, through Citizen Sense projects on fracking, traffic emissions (Dustbox, Deptford) and air-quality gardens; written as a how-to. — [Manifold edition](https://manifold.umn.edu/projects/citizens-of-worlds)
- **Ottinger, Gwen (2010).** "Buckets of Resistance: Standards and the Effectiveness of Citizen Science." *Science, Technology, & Human Values* 35(2), 244–270. doi:10.1177/0162243909337121. On "bucket" air samplers used by fenceline communities near refineries. Standards do two jobs at once: a "boundary-bridging" function that gives bucket data legitimacy with experts, and a "boundary-policing" function that lets experts dismiss the same data (e.g. a short grab sample set against a long-average standard). Standard-setting is therefore a site of intervention. — [Drexel record](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Buckets-of-Resistance-Standards-and-the/991021863493704721); [The Asthma Files annotation](https://theasthmafiles.org/node/132143). *Bearing:* the AQI's averaging periods are exactly this kind of standard. EPA's line that a one-minute sensor reading cannot be given an AQI colour is boundary-policing by averaging time; showing the averaging period on each value is the design response.
- **Calvillo, Nerea (2018).** "Political airs: From monitoring to attuned sensing air pollution." *Social Studies of Science* 48(3), 372–388. doi:10.1177/0306312718784656. Case: Madrid moved its monitoring stations in 2009, reported pollution fell, and a public controversy followed (lawsuits, blogs, flashmobs). Uses STS and feminist theory to propose "attuned sensing": publics come to sense air through the infrastructure and its politics, not only through its numbers. — [Warwick repository](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/103808). *Bearing:* direct evidence that *where the monitor is* changes the number, and that hiding monitor location is a political act.
- **Calvillo, Nerea (2018).** "Particular Sensibilities," *e-flux Architecture*, "Accumulation" series, October 2018. On *Yellow Dust* (see section 5). Notes that in the AQI gradient yellow means within legal limits, argues that vision-led displays reinforce seeing as the only way of knowing, and asks: "What if instead of making data visible, we make it sensible?" — [e-flux Architecture](https://www.e-flux.com/architecture/accumulation/217054/particular-sensibilities/)
- **Shapiro, Nicholas (2015).** "Attuning to the Chemosphere: Domestic Formaldehyde, Bodily Reasoning, and the Chemical Sublime." *Cultural Anthropology* 30(3), 368–393. doi:10.14506/ca30.3.02. Open access. Among residents of formaldehyde-emitting manufactured housing (former FEMA trailers), chronic low-level exposure is known through small, diffuse bodily signs. "Bodily reasoning" is the body's sensory knowledge; the "chemical sublime" is how accumulated minor irritations become grounds for ethical concern and action. — [Cultural Anthropology](https://journal.culanth.org/index.php/ca/article/view/1883). *Bearing:* the strongest theoretical warrant for treating a rescue-inhaler puff as a measurement.
- **Garnett, Emma (2016).** "Developing a feeling for error: Practices of monitoring and modelling air pollution data." *Big Data & Society* 3(2). doi:10.1177/2053951716658061. Ethnography of a UK public-health project (Weather Health and Air Pollution). Making a "good" measurement requires a practised feel for how instrument and air interact; checking for error is routine and interpretive. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/1881ad7fffb542c5b1552b065a953af3). See also Garnett (2017), "Air pollution in the making: Multiplicity and difference in interdisciplinary data practices," *Science, Technology, & Human Values* 42(5), 901–924 (cited in the search result, not opened). *Bearing:* even regulatory data is crafted and uncertain; showing `confidence` and `source` extends to the user a judgement scientists already make.
- **Kenner, Alison (2018).** *Breathtaking: Asthma Care in a Time of Climate Change.* University of Minnesota Press. Multi-sited US ethnography of asthma care. Chapters cover attuning to asthma in time and place, modes of control as care, breathing retraining, "the datafication of care" (asthma apps and sensors), and public-health "carescapes" under climate change. Argues for distributed, collective care rather than only individual management. — [Drexel announcement](https://drexel.edu/coas/news-events/news/2018/November/breathtaking-asthma-care-climate-change-ali-kenner); review by Jerry Zee, *Medical Anthropology Quarterly* 33(4), 2019 — [review page](https://medanthro.net/bookreview/review-of-breathtaking-asthma-care-in-a-time-of-climate/). *Bearing:* the essential critical counter-voice for this project. Kenner studied asthma apps directly; her worry is that tracking moves responsibility for polluted air onto the individual breather.
- **Fortun, Kim; Fortun, Mike; et al.** *The Asthma Files*, an ongoing collaborative digital ethnography of how asthma is understood and cared for in different places and disciplines. — [The Asthma Files](https://theasthmafiles.org/); related chapter: Fortun, M., Fortun, K., Costelloe-Kuehn, B., Saheb, T., Price, D., Kenner, A., & Crowder, J. (2014), "Asthma, Culture, and Cultural Analysis: Continuing Challenges," in *Advances in Experimental Medicine and Biology* (Springer), pp. 321–332 — [Drexel record](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/bookChapter/Asthma-Culture-and-Cultural-Analysis-Continuing/991019169519904721). Argues asthma is a knowledge problem and a cultural problem, and for "explanatory pluralism." *Bearing:* supports showing several partial explanations (pollutant, pollen, weather, place, the person's note) side by side instead of one cause.
- **Liboiron, Max (2021).** *Pollution Is Colonialism.* Duke University Press. Reviews confirm that a large part of the book examines "assimilative capacity," the idea that pollution is acceptable up to a threshold, traced to Streeter and Phelps's 1925 work on river self-purification, and argues threshold thinking grants a right to pollute and presumes access to Land as a sink. Verified through reviews, not the book. — [review in *Anthropologica*](https://id.erudit.org/iderudit/1109722ar); [Watershed Sentinel](https://watershedsentinel.ca/?p=23735). *Bearing:* the AQI's breakpoints are thresholds of this kind; "green" encodes permission. Liboiron also warns against replacing one threshold with another, which is a fair challenge to a "personal threshold."
- **Murphy, Michelle (2006).** *Sick Building Syndrome and the Problem of Uncertainty: Environmental Politics, Technoscience, and Women Workers.* Duke University Press. Traces how office workers, ventilation engineers, toxicologists and EPA made low-level indoor chemical exposure perceptible or imperceptible; uncertainty is produced, not just found. — [University of Toronto page](https://www.history.utoronto.ca/node/1269); [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/69450). *Bearing:* what a measurement regime cannot register (many small exposures, non-specific symptoms) becomes unreal. The index's exclusion of indoor air and non-criteria triggers is such a regime.
- **Choy, Timothy (2011).** "Air's Substantiations," chapter 6 (pp. 139–168) of *Ecologies of Comparison: An Ethnography of Endangerment in Hong Kong.* Duke University Press. On how air became a matter of political and medical concern in late-1990s Hong Kong. Chapter existence and pages verified; the argument was not read this session. — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/68722); [Duke University Press](https://dukeupress.edu/ecologies-of-comparison)

### Inferences
- A three-step argument is available from this literature: (1) thresholds and averaging rules are political (Ottinger, Liboiron, Murphy); (2) monitoring is situated and its siting changes the result (Calvillo, Garnett); (3) the body is already a sensor and its reasoning is knowledge (Shapiro, Kenner). The app's decomposition, provenance labels and personal comparison correspond to these three steps in order.
- Kenner and Liboiron give the thesis its most serious self-critique: personalising may individualise a collective harm, and a personal threshold is still a threshold. An honest defence names this and points to what the design does about it (plain statement that it is not medical advice; no risk percentage; language of resemblance, not prediction; the population threshold kept visible beside the personal one, not replaced).
- Gabrys's "just good enough data" is the best single citation for why a diary may show a 15-mile monitor reading at all, provided it says so.

### Gaps
- **Murphy, Michelle (2008).** "Chemical Regimes of Living." *Environmental History* 13(4), 695–703 — citation from memory; the DOI link failed (404) and search returned nothing. **Unverified.** As I recall it, the essay names the arrangements of regulation, production and science through which molecular relations become matters of life and concern.

## 5. Art and design precedents that represent air differently

### Takeaway
Precedents fall into four strategies, each un-flattening a different dimension: making the air *bodily* (Pollution Pods, Yellow Dust), making it *local and momentary* (Particle Falls, AirBeam, Dustbox, Feral Robotic Dogs), making its *politics and ownership* visible (Public Smog, Cloud Studies), and making sensing *collective* (Aerocene, Citizen Sense). Few of them address the individual's own body over time, which is the gap the thesis project occupies.

### Cited Findings

- **Michael Pinsky, *Pollution Pods* (2017).** Five interconnected geodesic domes whose air imitates Tautra (a clean Norwegian coastal site), London, New Delhi, Beijing and São Paulo; visitors walk through increasing pollution and changing temperature and humidity. First shown in Trondheim in 2017; commissioned by NTNU for Climart, a research project on whether art changes perception of climate change; shown at Somerset House, London, 18–25 April 2018. Sources disagree on whether there are five or six domes. — [artist's page](https://www.michaelpinsky.com/?p=3844); [Somerset House](https://www.somersethouse.org.uk/whats-on/michael-pinsky-pollution-pods); [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/pollution-pods-exhibit-replicates-air-some-worlds-most-polluted-cities-180968901/). *Reveals:* air as smell, haze and heat in the body; differences between places. *Hides:* each city is flattened to one typical recipe; time, neighbourhood and the visitor's own susceptibility disappear. An index turned into a room.
- **Andrea Polli, *Particle Falls* (first shown San José, 2010; Philadelphia as part of the Science History Institute's *Sensing Change*).** A building-scale projection of a blue waterfall; bright, fiery spots over it represent fine particulate detected by a nephelometer (Met One E-Sampler) beside the site, refreshed every 15 seconds. — [Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/particle-falls); debut year per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Polli) (secondary). *Reveals:* this street, these 15 seconds; no categories, no colour code, no threshold. *Hides:* composition and health meaning; one point stands for a place.
- **Nerea Calvillo with C+arquitectos / In the Air (Raúl Nieves, Pep Tornabell, Yee Thong Chai), *Yellow Dust* (2017).** Commissioned for the Seoul Biennale of Architecture and Urbanism 2017. Two low-cost sensors read particulates on site; the reading drives a cloud of water vapour, tinted yellow, that visitors feel on their skin and that also wets the air. Calvillo writes that the project "dissipates data" instead of decoding it, and deliberately uses yellow, which in the AQI gradient means within legal limits. — [Calvillo, e-flux Architecture, 2018](https://www.e-flux.com/architecture/accumulation/217054/particular-sensibilities/). *Reveals:* data as humidity and density on the body; the cultural coding of the index colours. *Hides:* numbers, on purpose. The most direct artistic critique of the AQI colour scale found.
- **Citizen Sense (Jennifer Gabrys et al.), *Dustbox* (2016–2018).** A particulate sensor (Shinyei optical sensor, Electric Imp wifi; board with Adrian McEwen, form with Francesca Perona) in a matte black 3D-printed ceramic shell whose shapes are based on electron-microscope views of pollen and carbon particles. Lent free from Deptford Lounge library, south London; deployed 29 October 2016 to April 2017. Data went to an open platform, was analysed with the Airsift toolkit, and assembled into neighbourhood "Data Stories." — [Citizen Sense](https://citizensense.net/kits/dustbox-hardware/). *Reveals:* street-level variation, the particle itself as form, and data joined to residents' accounts. *Hides:* less precise than reference monitors, which the project states and theorises ("just good enough").
- **HabitatMap (founded by Michael Heimbinder, 2006), *AirCasting* and *AirBeam*.** AirCasting is an open-source platform (app plus map) for recording and sharing measurements; AirBeam is a palm-sized particulate monitor. Mission: tools for communities facing disproportionate environmental burdens. — [HabitatMap](https://www.habitatmap.org/about). *Reveals:* the path of one person through a city; exposure as a trace, not a station. *Hides:* still translates readings into coloured bands on a map.
- **Natalie Jeremijenko, *Feral Robotic Dogs* (c. 2002 onward).** Commercial toy robot dogs rebuilt with chemical sensors and released in packs on sites of local concern (school grounds, parks, former industrial land); the pack follows the dog with the strongest reading, so its movement is the display. Open-source instructions; releases were staged as public and media events. — [ISEA archive](https://isea-archives.siggraph.org/?p=41308); [AP report, 2004](https://vindyarchives.com/news/2004/feb/15/robotic-dogs-a-nose-for-toxins). *Reveals:* gradient and direction (where is it worse?) through behaviour, with no number at all. *Hides:* quantity and compound.
- **Amy Balkin et al., *Public Smog* (2004–).** "A park in the atmosphere that fluctuates in location and scale," made by "purchasing and retiring emission offsets in regulated emissions markets." Lower Park opened over California's South Coast district in summer 2004; Upper Park over the EU from December 2006 to December 2007 and over the US April–August 2010; includes an effort to have Earth's atmosphere inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. The project page read does not name Balkin; the attribution is from prior knowledge. — [Public Smog](http://www.publicsmog.org/). *Reveals:* air as property governed by markets and permits, the same regulatory logic that anchors AQI 100 to a standard. *Hides:* nothing about bodies is shown; it is about governance.
- **Tomás Saraceno and the Aerocene community, *Aerocene* (app proposed 2012 from an MIT CAST residency; ongoing).** Solar-heated, fuel-free balloon sculptures and a backpack kit; the community lists sensing devices including a particulate sensor. Describes itself as "a movement for eco-social justice." *Aerocene Pacha* flew in January 2020 in Jujuy, Argentina. — [Aerocene](https://aerocene.org/). *Reveals:* air as a medium one can move with, and sensing as a shared practice. *Hides:* little health-specific content.
- **Forensic Architecture, *Cloud Studies*.** Not verified: the investigation page returned no text to the fetch tool. — [project URL (unverified content)](https://forensic-architecture.org/investigation/cloud-studies)

### Inferences
- A useful scheme for the thesis: every precedent chooses what to hide. Pinsky hides time and place within a city; Polli hides composition; Calvillo hides the number; Jeremijenko hides quantity; AirBeam hides health meaning; Balkin hides the body. None is "unflattened." The honest claim for the thesis project is that it *chooses differently* and shows its choices (source, distance, age), not that it escapes reduction.
- The projects closest to the thesis design are Dustbox/Data Stories (readings plus residents' accounts) and AirCasting (a personal trace). The thesis adds the comparison none of them make: this person's bad days against this person's ordinary days.
- *Yellow Dust* is the precedent to cite for the app's rule that colour is used only for the air and not as a verdict: Calvillo shows the AQI's yellow already carries a regulatory meaning ("within limits").
- Installations work at the scale of a visit; the diary works at the scale of months. That difference in duration is itself an argument: individual patterns are only legible over time.

### Gaps
All items below are **unverified this session** (search limit reached); details are from memory and must be checked at the artists' own pages before citing.
- **Preemptive Media (Beatriz da Costa, Brooke Singer, Jamie Schulte), *AIR — Area's Immediate Reading* (2006):** portable, networked air-monitoring devices lent to New Yorkers, showing CO, NOx and ozone readings and nearby polluters. The project domain pm-air.net now redirects to an unrelated site.
- **Nerea Calvillo et al., *In the Air* (2008, Medialab-Prado, Madrid):** a web visualisation rendering Madrid's monitored gases and particulates as a coloured topography over the city, with a prototype "diffuse façade" of coloured mist. intheair.es returned an error (503).

- **Olafur Eliasson:** *The weather project* (Tate Modern, 2003) and *Din blinde passager / Your blind passenger* (2010), fog as a medium that removes visual orientation.

## 6. Counter-argument: evidence the AQI works as risk communication, and why agencies prefer one number

### Takeaway
The AQI was created to end a real confusion (14 different indices in 55 cities) and there is causal evidence that alerts built on it change behaviour and protect health for some groups. The fair conclusion is that the index succeeds as a *broadcast* to a population and falls short as *information for one person*; the thesis should concede the first to argue the second.

### Cited Findings

- **The original problem was too many numbers.** A 1976 EPA/CEQ study found 55 urban areas using "14 different indices," which "sent a confusing message about air quality to the public." A single national index was the remedy. — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- **Simplicity was asked for by the agencies that issue the reports.** EPA added only one category in 1999 to "maintain the degree of simplicity strongly supported by State and local agencies," and fixed the colours so different agencies would not "send a confusing message." Focus groups preferred the AQI name and understood that "sensitive groups" does not mean the general public. — [64 FR 42530](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1999-08-04/html/99-19433.htm)
- **Alerts cause avoidance.** Neidell, Matthew (2009). "Information, Avoidance Behavior, and Health: The Effect of Ozone on Asthma Hospitalizations." *Journal of Human Resources* 44(2), 450–478. Regression-discontinuity design in Southern California: smog alerts significantly reduce daily attendance at two major outdoor facilities, and estimates of ozone's effect on asthma hospitalisations for children and the elderly are significantly larger once this avoidance is accounted for. Abstract-level verification. — [JHR](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/44/2/450); [NBER working paper 14209](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14209)
- **Awareness tracks exposure, and people with asthma respond more.** Awareness of alerts rises from 49.9% in counties with no days of AQI ≥ 101 to 61.3% with 15 or more; behaviour change rises from 10.2% to 23.1%; adults with asthma are more likely to have changed behaviour (24.6% vs 14.0%). — [Mirabelli et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182097/)
- **The AQI does track health when one pollutant dominates.** In California's cool season the AQI was associated with adult respiratory emergency visits in all regions (pooled RR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04); the authors conclude it can serve as a risk communication tool where the mixture is simple. — [Cromar et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7671501/)
- **In the Louisville inhaler-sensor data the AQI itself predicted rescue-inhaler use** (rate ratio 1.055 per IQR after adjusting for weather, significant in all sensitivity models). — [Su et al. 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5289907/)
- **Even the multi-pollutant alternative stayed a single number with a colour.** Stieb et al. present the AQHI as a promising "risk communication tool" used with health messages and a graphic display. — [EPA HERO record](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/620207)
- **EPA frames the real-time index as agency-giving:** current conditions give people "the power to take action when necessary to reduce outdoor activities and exposure." — [EPA TAD 2026](https://document.airnow.gov/technical-assistance-document-for-the-reporting-of-daily-air-quailty.pdf)
- **The limits of the counter-argument, from the same literature:** median adherence to alert advice is 31% (range 9.7–57%), and about half of US adults are not aware of alerts at all. — [D'Antoni et al. 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5610416/); [Mirabelli et al. 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7182097/)

### Inferences
- Reasons agencies prefer one number, assembled from the sources: legal duty to report daily to a whole metropolitan area; comparability between cities; a message that fits a weather segment or a road sign; one trigger for alerts and action days; and a direct tie to the standard the agency enforces. All are needs of the *sender*.
- The thesis claim that an index "takes agency from the person" should be narrowed to survive questioning. The evidence supports this version: the index gives a population an instruction (stay in, go out) and withholds the information a person would need to form their own judgement (which pollutant, how near, how recent, how it compares with their own history). Neidell shows the instruction works; D'Antoni shows many people fall back on their own senses anyway.
- A design that decomposes the index should keep a route back to the simple signal. The thesis app's single plain sentence over the components is defensible on this evidence: it keeps the benefit of a short message without making the short message the only thing available.
- The 2024 breakpoint change is a useful example for both sides: it shows the index is maintained against new science (in its favour) and that the colour is a policy setting, not a property of the air (in the thesis's favour).

### Gaps
- No experimental study was retrieved this session comparing comprehension of colour-coded scales against decomposed or numeric displays for air quality. Evidence that "simple colour scales improve comprehension" is here only as EPA's focus-group account, not as an independent trial.
- **Unverified leads:** Wen, X.-J., Balluz, L., & Mokdad, A. (2009), "Association between media alerts of air quality index and change of outdoor activity among adult asthma in six states, BRFSS, 2005," *Journal of Community Health* 34(1), 40–46, doi:10.1007/s10900-008-9126-4 (paywall blocked the fetch; likely the source of the 31%/16% figures quoted in D'Antoni et al.). Chen, H., et al. (2018), "Effect of air quality alerts on human health: a regression discontinuity analysis in Toronto, Canada," *The Lancet Planetary Health* 2(1), e19–e26 (I recall it finding little overall health benefit from alerts, which would cut against the counter-argument; not confirmed). Saberian, S., Heyes, A., & Rivers, N. (2017), "Alerts work! Air quality warnings and cycling," *Resource and Energy Economics* 49 (Sydney).

- No source was found on whether more detailed displays cause alarm fatigue or worse decisions for lay users, which is the obvious objection to the thesis design and should be answered from the risk-communication literature (e.g. work on graphic formats and numeracy) in a further search.
