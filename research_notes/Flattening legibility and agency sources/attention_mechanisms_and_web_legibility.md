## A1. Foundational books and essays on attention capture (Schüll, Fogg, Eyal, Raskin, Harris, Williams, Wu, Zuboff, Odell, Terranova, Citton, Simon)

### Takeaway
The line from slot machines to feeds is well documented in the literature, and the strongest verified anchors are Simon (1971), Williams (2018, open access) and Grosser's own paper on metrics. Schüll, Fogg, Eyal, Wu, Zuboff, Odell, Citton and Terranova are real and standard, but could not be re-opened at their publishers this session.

### Cited Findings
- **[V] Simon, Herbert A. (1971). "Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World." In M. Greenberger (ed.), *Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest*. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins Press.** Claim: information consumes attention, so in an information-rich world attention is the scarce resource and systems must be judged by how they allocate it. Quote: "Hence a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention". Relation: supports. It is the origin of the "attention economy" frame, and it is about design of systems that filter, which is the thesis's "indexing". Note: the page range is disputed. Sources give pp. 37-52 or pp. 40-41 for the quote; the "37-72" range in the brief found no support. — [scanned chapter](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/design/1971-simon.pdf); [CMU digital collections record](https://digitalcollections.library.cmu.edu/node/63935); [commentary with page refs](https://conversableeconomist.com/2015/08/17/economics-of-information-overload-thoughts-from-herb-simon/)
- **[V] Williams, James (2018). *Stand Out of Our Light: Freedom and Resistance in the Attention Economy*. Cambridge University Press. DOI 10.1017/9781108453004. Open access, CC BY-NC 4.0.** Claim: the attention economy's design goals compete with the user's own goals, and the harm is to the capacity to want what one wants to want, not only to time. Publisher quote: "As information becomes ever more plentiful, the resource that is becoming more scarce is our attention." Relation: supports, and gives the thesis a philosophical account of agency (attention as a precondition of freedom). — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/stand-out-of-our-light/3F8D7BA2C0FE3A7126A4D9B73A89415D)
- **[V] Grosser, Benjamin (2014). "What Do Metrics Want? How Quantification Prescribes Social Interaction on Facebook." *Computational Culture*, Issue 4, 9 November 2014.** Claim: visible counts (likes, friends, timestamps) prescribe behaviour and feed a "desire for more"; hiding them shows how much they steer. Quote: "The primary thing that Facebook metrics want is increased user engagement with the site." Relation: supports, and is the closest scholarly precedent for an artwork-plus-essay pair like the author's. — [Computational Culture](https://computationalculture.net/what-do-metrics-want/)
- **[V] Weiser, Mark (1991). "The Computer for the 21st Century." *Scientific American* 265(3): 94-104.** Quote: "The most profound technologies are those that disappear." Relation: this is the design ideal the thesis argues against. Disappearance is presented as a virtue; the thesis reads it as the removal of the person's view of the mechanism. — [reprint at calmtech.com](https://calmtech.com/papers/computer-for-the-21st-century); [excerpt with citation](https://webpages.charlotte.edu/richter/classes/2006/6010/readings/WeiserSciAm.htm)
- **Aza Raskin on infinite scroll: only secondary sources verified.** Raskin is credited with inventing infinite scroll in 2006. The "behavioural cocaine" line and his regret are widely repeated, but the primary BBC Panorama article (July 2018) could not be opened this session, so the wording is not confirmed. Harry Brignull's book chapter on exploiting addiction discusses Raskin and is a citable secondary source. — [Brignull, Deceptive Patterns, ch. 11](https://deceptive.design/book/contents/chapter-11); [Masters of Scale video page](https://mastersofscale.com/video/he-invented-infinite-scroll-and-says-he-regrets-it/)

### Inferences
- Simon and Weiser bracket the argument well. Simon says the designer's job is to protect attention by filtering. Weiser says the designer's job is to make the technology vanish. Feeds do both at once: they filter for the platform's goals and hide that they are doing it.
- Williams is the safest "agency" citation because it is open access and the author can quote it directly.

### Gaps
- **[M] Schüll, Natasha Dow (2012). *Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas*. Princeton University Press.** The "machine zone" and the removal of stopping points are the book's known argument, but the Princeton UP page returned 403 and no quote was verified. Confirm edition, ISBN and page numbers in the book itself.
- **[M] Fogg, B.J. (2003). *Persuasive Technology: Using Computers to Change What We Think and Do*. Morgan Kaufmann**; and Fogg (2009), "A Behavior Model for Persuasive Design", Persuasive '09 (DOI believed to be 10.1145/1541948.1541999, unverified).
- **[M] Eyal, Nir (2014). *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products*. Portfolio.** The four-step hook with variable reward. Not opened.
- **[M] Wu, Tim (2016). *The Attention Merchants*. Knopf. Zuboff, Shoshana (2019). *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*. PublicAffairs. Odell, Jenny (2019). *How to Do Nothing*. Melville House. Citton, Yves (2017). *The Ecology of Attention*. Polity (French original 2014). Terranova, Tiziana (2012). "Attention, Economy and the Brain." *Culture Machine* 13.** All standard, none re-verified this session.
- **[M] Tristan Harris**: "How Technology Is Hijacking Your Mind" (Medium, 2016) and the Center for Humane Technology (formerly Time Well Spent). Not opened.

## A2. Dark patterns and attention-capture patterns: the HCI evidence

### Takeaway
There is now a peer-reviewed vocabulary for exactly what the color scroller shows. Monge Roffarello et al. (CHI 2023) name infinite scroll as one of eleven "attention capture damaging patterns", and Lukoff et al. and Baughan et al. tie specific interface features to a measured loss of sense of agency.

### Cited Findings
- **[V-bib] Monge Roffarello, A., Lukoff, K., & De Russis, L. (2023). "Defining and Identifying Attention Capture Deceptive Designs in Digital Interfaces." CHI '23, article 194. DOI 10.1145/3544548.3580729.** Claim: a systematic review of 43 papers that defines attention capture damaging patterns and identifies eleven types, including infinite scroll; earlier dark-pattern typologies covered money and privacy but not attention. Relation: supports. It gives the thesis a citable name for the mechanism and shows the field treats it as a design choice, not a neutral default. — [Politecnico di Torino repository](https://iris.polito.it/handle/11583/2978380); [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/chi/RoffarelloLR23)
- **[V-bib] Lukoff, K., Lyngs, U., Zade, H., Liao, J.V., Choi, J., Fan, K., Munson, S.A., & Hiniker, A. (2021). "How the Design of YouTube Influences User Sense of Agency." CHI '21, article 368. DOI 10.1145/3411764.3445467.** Claim: in a survey of 120 U.S. users, autoplay and recommendations were the features that most undermined sense of agency, and search and playlists supported it. Co-design sessions showed that people with a specific intention prefer agency-supporting interfaces. Relation: supports, with a useful complication: people sometimes want low-agency use. — [arXiv 2101.11778](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.11778); [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:31bc88e5-f9ff-437f-a22b-533ea2d0f897)
- **[V-bib] Baughan, A., et al. (2022). "'I Don't Even Remember What I Read': How Design Influences Dissociation on Social Media." CHI '22. DOI 10.1145/3491102.3501899.** Claim: with a custom Twitter client (Chirp) used by 43 U.S. participants, the authors frame mindless scrolling as normative dissociation (absorption with reduced self-awareness and agency) and argue this is a more productive frame than addiction. Absorption was sometimes a welcome break. Relation: complicates. It supports "the design removes awareness" but opposes the word "addiction". Note: sources disagree on the prompt timing (three minutes vs fifteen), so check the paper. — [University of Washington news](https://www.washington.edu/news/2022/05/23/people...); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/953637)
- **[V-bib] Lukoff, K., Yu, C., Kientz, J., & Hiniker, A. (2018). "What Makes Smartphone Use Meaningful or Meaningless?" *Proc. ACM IMWUT* 2(1), article 22. DOI 10.1145/3191754.** Claim: 45 users over two weeks; habitual use was rated less meaningful than intentional use and came with a felt loss of autonomy, though some "meaningless" use worked as a micro escape. Relation: supports the agency claim, complicates any claim that all passive use is harm. — [author PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/meaninglessPhoneUse.pdf)
- **[V-bib] Gray, C.M., Kou, Y., Battles, B., Hoggatt, J., & Toombs, A.L. (2018). "The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design." CHI '18. DOI 10.1145/3173574.3174108.** Claim: a content analysis of practitioner-identified dark patterns, with categories such as nagging and obstruction, and a warning that designers become complicit in manipulation. Relation: supports; it locates the hiding in professional practice. — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-dark-patterns-side-of-ux-design/); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10057203)
- **[V-bib] Mathur, A., Acar, G., Friedman, M.J., Lucherini, E., Mayer, J., Chetty, M., & Narayanan, A. (2019). "Dark Patterns at Scale: Findings from a Crawl of 11K Shopping Websites." *Proc. ACM HCI* 3 (CSCW), article 81. DOI 10.1145/3359183.** Claim: a crawl of about 53K product pages on about 11K shopping sites found 1,818 dark pattern instances in 15 types and 7 categories; 183 sites used deceptive practices, and 22 third parties sell dark patterns as a service. Relation: supports with scale: the patterns are an industry supply chain, not isolated bad actors. — [arXiv 1907.07032](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07032v2); [Princeton project page](https://webtransparency.cs.princeton.edu/dark-patterns)
- **[V] Brignull, Harry. *Deceptive Patterns* (site and book, formerly darkpatterns.org).** The site hosts a library of the research above and Brignull's book chapter on exploiting addiction. — [deceptive.design](https://deceptive.design/book/contents/chapter-11)
- **[V-bib] Holte, A.J., & Ferraro, F.R. (2020). "True colors: Grayscale setting reduces screen time in college students." *The Social Science Journal*. DOI 10.1080/03623319.2020.1737461.** Claim: 161 undergraduates; those who switched to grayscale for 8-10 days cut daily screen time by about 38 minutes (37.90), with less social media and browsing but no change in video; anxiety and depression changes were not attributable to grayscale. Relation: supports the claim that colour is a working part of the attention mechanism, which is the color scroller's premise. A University of Cincinnati news item gives slightly different numbers (39 minutes, about 220 students); use the paper's. — [abstract record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1349793630393233408); [UC news](https://uc.edu/news/articles/2022/10/n21124601.html)
- **Later grayscale work is mixed.** A 2023 study in *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* (DOI 10.1016/j.chbr.2023.100294) tested grayscale as a self-nudge, and a 2023 SAGE article reviewing earlier studies noted the effect on social media use is not consistent across studies. — [SAGE article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20501579231212062)

### Inferences
- The author can say that infinite scroll is a named, catalogued "damaging pattern" in the CHI literature, and that colour removal has a measured (if modest and not always replicated) effect on use. Both claims are defensible if stated at that strength.
- Baughan et al. offer a better word than addiction for what the scroller shows: absorption with the stopping point taken away.

### Gaps
- "Stopping cues" as a named concept: the popular source is Adam Alter, *Irresistible* (2017) **[M]**. No empirical paper using that exact term was verified. The search for it did not run (budget exhausted).
- **[M]** Lyngs et al., "Self-Control in Cyberspace" (CHI 2019) and "I Just Want to Hack Myself to Not Get Distracted" (CHI 2020, on removing the Facebook feed) are relevant and believed real; DOIs not checked.
- Titles and authors of the two 2023 grayscale papers were not confirmed; only the DOIs surfaced.

## A3. Is "digital addiction" real? The sceptics

### Takeaway
The word "addiction" is contested. The best large-sample evidence finds very small associations between technology use and well-being, and a review in the addiction field itself argues "problematic use" is the accurate term. The thesis is safer arguing about designed loss of agency than about addiction as a diagnosis.

### Cited Findings
- **[V-bib] Orben, A., & Przybylski, A.K. (2019). "The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use." *Nature Human Behaviour* 3: 173-182. DOI 10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1.** Claim: across three large datasets (over 300,000 adolescents) and using specification curve analysis, technology use explains at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being; analytic choices can flip the sign of the result. Relation: opposes strong harm claims. The potato comparison is press framing, not the paper's wording. — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:5d844350-a359-47d3-b10c-4bfb93ce613b); [Scientific American coverage](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-kids-who-use-tech-seem-to-be-all-right/?text=The)
- **[V] Panova, T., & Carbonell, X. (2018). "Is smartphone addiction really an addiction?" *Journal of Behavioral Addictions* 7(2): 252-259. DOI 10.1556/2006.7.2018.49.** Claim: a review finds insufficient support for "smartphone addiction" as an addiction; similar presentation does not make a behaviour an addiction; "problematic use" is proposed, to be studied in sociocultural context. Relation: opposes the word in the thesis's framing, supports looking at context and design. — [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174603)
- **Kardefelt-Winther et al. (2017), in *Addiction*,** proposed that a behavioural addiction needs significant functional impairment or distress as a direct consequence, and persistence over time. This is reported second-hand through Panova & Carbonell. — [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174603)
- Baughan et al. (above) argue from inside HCI that dissociation is a more productive frame than addiction. — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/953637)

### Inferences
- A committee member who knows this literature will ask about "addiction". A defensible answer: the work exposes mechanisms that designers borrowed from, or that resemble, machine gambling (removed stopping points, variable reward, colour), and the claim is about hidden design and reduced sense of agency, which Lukoff and Baughan measured, not about clinical addiction.

### Gaps
- Kardefelt-Winther et al.'s own page was not opened (believed: *Addiction* 112(10): 1709-1715, DOI 10.1111/add.13763, **[M]**).
- **[M]** Orben (2020), "The Sisyphean Cycle of Technology Panics", *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, is a useful sceptical essay; not verified.

## A4. Design ideas: seamless vs seamful, friction, slow and calm technology

### Takeaway
"Seamful design", "design frictions" and "slow technology" are the established design-research terms for what the author's work does: putting the seam, the pause and the mechanism back in view.

### Cited Findings
- **[V-bib] Chalmers, M., & Galani, A. (2004). "Seamful interweaving: heterogeneity in the theory and design of interactive systems." DIS '04, pp. 243-252. DOI 10.1145/1013115.1013149.** Claim: designing for a single seamless medium clashes with everyday activity, which mixes many media; seams can be a resource. Relation: supports; gives a positive design programme for revealing. — [Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/3457)
- **[V] Chalmers, M., & MacColl, I. "Seamful and Seamless Design in Ubiquitous Computing."** Questions the assumption that seamless integration must be the goal. Venue and year were not confirmed by the records found (a 2003 workshop paper is likely). — [author draft PDF](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~matthew/papers/Eurowearable2003Draft5.pdf)
- **[V-bib] Cox, A.L., Gould, S.J.J., Cecchinato, M.E., Iacovides, I., & Renfree, I. (2016). "Design Frictions for Mindful Interactions: The Case for Microboundaries." CHI EA '16, pp. 1389-1397. DOI 10.1145/2851581.2892410.** Claim: deliberately added friction interrupts automatic, mindless interaction and prompts reflection. Relation: supports; "frictionless" is a choice with a cost. — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1475258/1/Design%20Frictions_CHI2016LBW_v18.camera.ready.pdf); [Open University record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/47011)
- **[V-bib] Hallnäs, L., & Redström, J. (2001). "Slow Technology – Designing for Reflection." *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing* 5(3): 201-212. DOI 10.1007/PL00000019.** Claim: a design agenda for technology aimed at reflection and mental rest instead of efficiency. Relation: supports. — [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FPL00000019)
- **[V] Weiser, M., & Brown, J.S. (1995). "Designing Calm Technology." Xerox PARC, 21 December 1995.** Quote: "Calm technology engages both the center and the periphery of our attention". Relation: complicates. Calm technology hides things to protect attention, the opposite motive from engagement design but the same move of removing things from view. — [calmtech.com](https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology)

### Inferences
- The thesis should separate two kinds of hiding: hiding to protect the person's attention (calm technology, progressive disclosure) and hiding to capture it (feeds). The test is whether the person can bring the hidden thing back into view when they want.

### Gaps
- Weiser's own use of "seamful" and "beautiful seams" (often cited through Chalmers) was not found at a primary source.
- "Frictionless as ideology": no single verified source. Cox et al. is the nearest scholarly anchor.

## A5. Art and design that exposes the mechanisms (projects)

### Takeaway
Ben Grosser's work is the closest precedent family, and Rafaël Rozendaal's single-purpose colour websites are the closest formal precedent for pure colour fields in the browser. No verified work was found that combines pure colour fields with infinite scroll as a critique of feeds, which suggests the color scroller's combination is distinctive.

### Cited Findings
- **[V] Ben Grosser, *Facebook Demetricator*, 2012-2022.** Browser extension. Quote: "Facebook Demetricator is a web browser extension that hides these metrics." Hides the counts, reveals how much they drive behaviour. — [bengrosser.com](https://bengrosser.com/projects/facebook-demetricator/)
- **[V] Ben Grosser, *The Endless Doomscroller*, first shown 2020.** An endless, auto-scrolling feed of generic doom headlines with the specifics removed. Hides content, reveals the scroll mechanism itself. This is the nearest verified precedent to the color scroller. — [project page](https://bengrosser.com/projects/endless-doomscroller/); [the work](https://endlessdoomscroller.com)
- **[V] Ben Grosser, *Minus*, 2021.** Quote: "a finite social network where users get only 100 posts—for life". Reverses the infinite: scarcity made visible as the only metric. — [project page](https://bengrosser.com/projects/minus/); [minus.social](https://minus.social)
- **[V] Ben Grosser, *Safebook*, press from 2018.** Quote: "Safebook is Facebook without the content, a browser extension that hides all images, text, video, and audio on the site." Hides everything except the interface, so the interface becomes the subject. — [bengrosser.com](https://bengrosser.com/projects/safebook/)
- **[V] Ben Grosser, *Go Rando*, 2017 (v2 2021).** Quote: "Every time you click “Like”, Go Rando randomly chooses one of the seven “reactions” for you." Obfuscates the emotional data the platform collects. — [bengrosser.com](https://bengrosser.com/projects/go-rando/)
- **[V] Ben Grosser, *Twitter Demetricator*.** Hides all metrics on Twitter. Existence confirmed on the artist's GitHub; the year (believed 2018) was not confirmed. — [GitHub profile](https://github.com/bengrosser)
- **[V] Moniker (Luna Maurer, Roel Wouters; Jonathan Puckey until 2016) with VPRO Medialab, *Clickclickclick.click*, 2016.** A white page with one green button that narrates every cursor movement and click back to the visitor. Reveals behavioural tracking that is normally silent. — [studiomoniker.com](https://studiomoniker.com/projects/click-click-click); [VPRO](https://www.vprobroadcast.com/medialab/news/2016/clickclickclick.click.html)
- **[V] Moniker, *Do Not Touch*, 2013.** Crowdsourced interactive music video (Light Light, "Kilo") that records and replays visitors' cursors. Makes the collected trace of each user visible. — [studiomoniker.com](https://studiomoniker.com/projects/do-not-touch)
- **[V] Joana Moll, *The Hidden Life of an Amazon User*, 2019 (commissioned by IMPAKT, EMARE programme).** One book purchase on 17 June 2019 produced 1,307 requests, 8,724 A4 pages of code and 87.33 MB across twelve interfaces. The visitor scrolls through the code. Reveals the code and energy cost under a "one click" interface; it belongs to both topic A and B. — [project site](https://www.janavirgin.com/AMZ/)
- **[V] Dries Depoorter with David Surprenant, *Die With Me*, 2018 (IDFA DocLab).** Quote: "Chat app you can only use when you have less than 5% battery." A hard stopping cue built into the app. — [driesdepoorter.be](https://driesdepoorter.be/diewithme/)
- **[V] Sam Lavigne and Tega Brain, *The Good Life (Enron Simulator)*, 2016, Rhizome microgrant; *New York Apartment*, 2020, Whitney artport.** The Good Life delivers the Enron email archive to your inbox over years: volume and duration made felt. — [lav.io](https://lav.io/projects/the-good-life-enron-simulator/); [Rhizome](https://old.rhizome.org/editorial/2017/sep/06/the-good-life)
- **[V] Rafaël Rozendaal, websites, 2001-present (about 230 listed).** Verified titles, years and domains relevant to colour: colorflip.com (2008), intotime.com (2010), intotime.org (2011), intotime.us (2012), pinkyellowblue.com (2014), slickquick.com (2014), abstractbrowsing.net (2014), and "Text Free Browsing" (2013). The listing gives no descriptions, so what each does should be checked by opening it. — [newrafael.com/internet](https://www.newrafael.com/internet/)
- **[V] *Humane by Design*.** Quote: "Guidance for designing humane digital products and services focused around digital well-being." Principles listed include Empowering, Finite, Inclusive. The page does not name its maker. — [humanebydesign.com](https://humanebydesign.com/)
- **[V] Chirp (research artefact, Baughan et al. 2022).** A custom Twitter client with reading-history labels, time-limit dialogs and usage statistics, built to test stopping cues. — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/953637)

### Inferences
- Grosser's method is subtraction: remove one layer (numbers, content, specifics) so the remaining layer becomes visible. The color scroller can be positioned as the same method applied to colour and motion.
- Rozendaal's *Abstract Browsing* is believed to be a browser extension that turns web pages into fields of flat colour **[M]**. If confirmed, it is the nearest formal precedent for "the web as colour" and should be cited next to the color scroller.

All are believed real **[M]**.
- **Laura Dornheim**: not confirmed as an artist or designer in this field. No source found; the name may be a mix-up. Do not cite.
- **Olia Lialina** as an artist (as opposed to essayist) and "infinite scroll" artworks by other artists were not searched.
- No verified work combining pure colour fields with scrolling as critique was found. This is an absence in the search, not proof that none exists.

## B1. "View source" as a literacy, and the handmade or small web

### Takeaway
There is a coherent body of practitioner essays arguing that hand-made, readable websites are a form of agency and that industrial web practice took it away. The best verified quotes come from Carpenter, Schwulst, Sloan, Lialina, Satyal and Russell.

### Cited Findings
- **[V] Carpenter, J.R. (2015). "A Handmade Web." Presented at *Slow Media*, Bath Spa University, 26 March 2015; printed in *Uniformagazine* no. 5, January 2016.** Claim: the handmade web is pages coded by hand and kept by individuals, linked to zines and artists' books, and a form of resistance. Quotes: "web pages coded by hand rather than by software"; "slowness and smallness as a forms of resistance" (sic). Relation: supports. — [luckysoap.com](https://luckysoap.com/statements/handmadeweb.html)
- **[V] Schwulst, Laurel (21 May 2018). "My website is a shifting house next to a river of knowledge. What could yours be?" *The Creative Independent*.** Claim: a website is a personal, unfinished space, and making one is how individuals shape the web instead of leaving it to corporations. Quote: "Each artist should create their own space on the web, for a website is an individual act of collective ambition." Relation: supports. — [The Creative Independent](https://thecreativeindependent.com/essays/laurel-schwulst-my-website-is-a-shifting-house-next-to-a-river-of-knowledge-what-could-yours-be/)
- **[V] Sloan, Robin (February 2020). "An app can be a home-cooked meal."** Claim: software made for a few people, outside commercial and scaling pressure, is a different activity from industrial software. Quote: "I am the programming equivalent of a home cook." Relation: supports, and also complicates: Sloan had to be able to program to have this agency. — [robinsloan.com](https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/home-cooked-app/)
- **[V] Lialina, Olia (2005). "A Vernacular Web: The Indigenous and The Barbarians."** Claim: the amateur web of the 1990s was not erased; it was buried by search rankings and neglect. Quote: "The amateur web didn't die and it has not disappeared but it is hidden." Relation: supports the indexing half of the thesis directly: ranking decides what is visible. — [art.teleportacia.org](https://art.teleportacia.org/observation/vernacular/)
- **[V] Lialina, Olia (October 2012). "Turing Complete User."** Claim: the "invisible computer" also makes the user invisible; replacing "users" with "people" hides the line between those who build systems and those who use them, and that line is where users' rights come from. She proposes the General Purpose User. Quote: "There is nothing one user can do, that another can't given enough time and respect." The essay quotes Don Norman: "The real problem with the interface is that it is an interface". Relation: supports; this is the single most on-point essay for topic B. — [contemporary-home-computing.org](http://contemporary-home-computing.org/turing-complete-user/)
- **[V] Satyal, Parimal (2 November 2017). "Against an Increasingly User-Hostile Web."** Claim: the open web is being replaced by one that restricts and commodifies. His own test of one news article: about 3.1 MB and about 460 requests, of which the article was under 6% of the data. Quote: "We're quietly replacing an open web that connects and empowers with one that restricts and commoditizes people." Relation: supports, with the author's own (single-page, 2017) measurements. — [neustadt.fr](https://neustadt.fr/essays/against-a-user-hostile-web/)

## B2. Theory: generativity, software as ideology, platforms and infrastructure, the right to see and modify

### Takeaway
The theoretical case splits into three strands the author can cite: interfaces hide the machine (Kittler, Chun, Emerson), the open web was re-engineered into platform infrastructure (Helmond, Plantin et al., Zittrain), and people should be able to see and reshape their tools (Victor, Litt and Ink & Switch, Illich).

## B3. Concrete mechanisms of web illegibility, with measurements

### Takeaway
HTTP Archive's Web Almanac gives citable numbers: the median home page tripled in weight on mobile in a decade and now ships roughly 650-700 KB of JavaScript, about half of it unused at load, and only about a fifth of pages expose source maps. Pew measured that AI summaries roughly halve clicks through to sources.

### Cited Findings
- **[V] Median page weight, July 2025:** "The median home page in 2025 was 2.86 MB on desktop and 2.56 MB on mobile." In July 2015 the median mobile home page was 845 KB, and the chapter reports a 202.8% increase over the decade. Caution: the chapter's body text also gives 2,362 KB for mobile, which conflicts with its own figures; 2,559 KB matches the stated increase. — [Web Almanac 2025, Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/page-weight)
- **[V] JavaScript per page, 2025:** "The median desktop page uses 708 KB of JavaScript", and 646 KB on mobile. — [Web Almanac 2025, Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/page-weight)
- **[V] Requests, 2025:** "The median page makes 77 requests on desktop and 72 on mobile." (A figure in the same chapter says 73 and 68.) — [Web Almanac 2025, Page Weight](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/page-weight)
- **[V] JavaScript, 2024:** the median payload rose 14% to 558 KB on mobile and 613 KB on desktop. Median unused JavaScript on mobile was 206 KB, or 44% of bytes delivered. — [Web Almanac 2024, JavaScript](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/javascript)
- **[V] Source maps, 2024:** 19% of mobile pages point to publicly accessible source maps. This is the nearest available measure of how much shipped code can be read in its original form. webpack was detected on about 5% of sites, Babel on 6.1% of mobile sites (12% of the top 10,000), TypeScript on about 6%; these detection figures are floors, since bundling often leaves no trace. — [Web Almanac 2024, JavaScript](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/javascript)
- **[V] Moll's single Amazon purchase:** 1,307 requests, 8,724 A4 pages of code, 87.33 MB. — [The Hidden Life of an Amazon User](https://www.janavirgin.com/AMZ/)
- **[V] Low-tech Magazine's comparison:** the average page among the 500,000 most popular sites grew from 0.45 MB in 2010 to 1.7 MB in June 2018. — [Low-tech Magazine, About the solar website](https://solar.lowtechmagazine.com/about/the-solar-website/)
- **[V] AI answers replacing links (Pew Research Center, 22 July 2025):** 900 U.S. adults, browsing data for March 2025, 68,879 Google searches of which about 18% produced an AI summary. "Users who encountered an AI summary clicked on a traditional search result link in 8% of all visits", against 15% without one. Only 1% of visits included a click on a link inside the summary. Sessions ended after 26% of pages with a summary and 16% without. Google disputed the study. — [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/); [report of Google's response](https://ppc.land/google-disputes-pew-study-showing-ai-overviews-reduce-clicks-by-half/)
- **Mixed evidence on zero-click search:** Semrush found the zero-click rate fell slightly (33.75% to 31.53%) for the same keywords after AI Overviews appeared, against the Pew direction. This is an SEO-industry source. — [Semrush](https://semrush.com/blog/semrush-ai-overviews-study)

### Inferences
- The Pew figure is the strongest evidence for the "indexing flattens" claim: an answer layer sits on top of the index, and one visit in a hundred follows a cited source.
- Page weight alone does not prove illegibility (images are the largest share, 1,059 KB on desktop). The JavaScript and source-map figures carry the legibility argument better.

### Gaps
- No Web Almanac figure was obtained for WebAssembly use, minification rates, or obfuscated (hashed) class names. The 2025 JavaScript chapter URL returned 404.
- DRM/EME, AMP, app webviews and cookie banners: no sources gathered (search budget exhausted). From memory **[M]**: EME became a W3C Recommendation in 2017 over objections including the EFF's; AMP launched in 2015.

## B4. "Legibility" in Scott's sense and in type, and illegible spaces online

### Takeaway
Rao's 2010 essay is the standard bridge from Scott to technology culture, and the "dark forest" and "cozyweb" essays describe people choosing illegibility to platforms. No verified source was found that explicitly joins typographic legibility to Scott's political legibility for the web, so that link may be the author's own contribution.

### Cited Findings
- **[V] Rao, Venkatesh (26 July 2010). "A Big Little Idea Called Legibility." *Ribbonfarm*.** Claim: summarising Scott's *Seeing Like a State*, the failure pattern begins when an observer cannot comprehend a complex reality and, treating that as the reality's fault, imposes a simplified, legible replacement. Quote: the failure begins when you "Attribute that failure to the irrationality of what you are looking at, rather than your own limitations." Relation: supports and sharpens: legibility here means legible to the governing system, at the cost of the governed. — [ribbonfarm.com](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/2010/07/26/a-big-little-idea-called-legibility/)
- **[V] Strickler, Yancey (May 2019). "The Dark Forest Theory of the Internet."** Claim: people are withdrawing from open, indexed platforms into private, unindexed spaces. Quotes: "This is also what the internet is becoming: a dark forest."; "The dark forests grow because they provide psychological and reputational cover." Relation: complicates. Withdrawal is agency, but he warns it leaves the public web to the platforms. — [ystrickler.com](https://www.ystrickler.com/the-dark-forest-theory-of-the-internet/)
- **[V] Rao, Venkatesh (24 May 2019). "The Extended Internet Universe."** Introduces the cozyweb. Quote: "More and more civilian online activity is sinking into the cozyweb." — [contraptions.venkateshrao.com](https://contraptions.venkateshrao.com/p/the-extended-internet-universe)
- **[V] Appleton, Maggie (2023). "The Expanding Dark Forest and Generative AI."** Claim: language models will fill the public web with generated text, and people will have to prove they are human. Quote: "Our new challenge as little snowflake humans will be to prove we aren’t language models." Relation: supports; extends flattening to generated content. — [maggieappleton.com](https://maggieappleton.com/ai-dark-forest)

### Inferences
- The word "legibility" points in opposite directions in the two literatures, and the thesis can use that. The old web was legible to the person (view source) and illegible to the platform. - The dark forest essays show the person's remaining move: become illegible to the index.

### Gaps

## B6. Counter-arguments: abstraction, usability and simplicity

### Takeaway
The strongest counter-position is that hiding is what makes tools usable, and it has reputable sources. The thesis has to answer it; the available answer is that the question is who controls the hiding and whether it can be reversed.

### Cited Findings
- **[V] Nielsen, Jakob (3 December 2006). "Progressive Disclosure." Nielsen Norman Group.** Quote: "Progressive disclosure defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen", making applications easier to learn and less error-prone. Relation: opposes, in part. Note that progressive disclosure keeps the hidden features reachable ("only if a user asks for them"), which a feed's ranking logic is not. — [nngroup.com](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- **[V] Don Norman, quoted in Lialina (2012):** "The real problem with the interface is that it is an interface". Norman's *The Invisible Computer* position is that the tool should get out of the way of the task. Relation: opposes. The quote was read in Lialina's essay, not in Norman's text. — [Turing Complete User](http://contemporary-home-computing.org/turing-complete-user/)
- **[V] Weiser (1991)** and **Weiser & Brown (1995)**: disappearance and calm as goals; see A1 and A4. — [calmtech.com](https://calmtech.com/papers/designing-calm-technology)
- **[V] Lukoff et al. (2021)**: people with no specific intention sometimes prefer low-agency features, so not everyone wants control all the time. — [arXiv 2101.11778](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.11778)
- **[V] Laurie Voss, "The case for frameworks"**, a direct reply to Russell: frameworks and abstraction let more people build more. — [seldo.com](https://seldo.com/posts/the_case_for_frameworks)
- **[V] Litt (2023)**: language models, an opaque layer, may let many more people write small programs. — [geoffreylitt.com](https://www.geoffreylitt.com/2023/03/25/llm-end-user-programming.html)
- **[V] Orben & Przybylski (2019)** and **Panova & Carbonell (2018)**: the harm is smaller and less "addiction"-like than the popular account; see A3. — [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6174603)

### Inferences
- A workable reply to the usability objection: Nielsen's progressive disclosure and Weiser's calm both assume the person can call the hidden layer forward. Compiled bundles without source maps (81% of mobile pages) and ranking systems with no inspectable logic do not offer that. The thesis is against irreversible hiding done in someone else's interest, not against simplicity.
- The honest concession: most people never viewed source. The argument is about the option existing and about who learned from it, not about majority behaviour.

### Gaps
- **[M], not checked:** Norman, *Living with Complexity* (MIT Press, 2010) and *The Invisible Computer* (MIT Press, 1998); Maeda, *The Laws of Simplicity* (MIT Press, 2006).
- No empirical figure was found for how many people ever used "view source".
