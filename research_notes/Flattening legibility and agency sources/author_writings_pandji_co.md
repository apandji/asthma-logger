**Essay 1: "The Legible Web" (May 7, 2026) — the essay on web technologies and legibility**
- URL and tags: tagged agency · affordances · legibility · materiality; also links to idea pages "surveillance capitalism" and "hidden affordance" — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Opening frame: Berners-Lee's one-way `<a>` link versus Ted Nelson's two-way link (Xanadu, "transclusion"). "The two-way link, with every connection being visible, auditable, and legible to every user, encodes a certain politics into the technology itself." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Central claim about asymmetry: the one-way link's "asymmetrical relationship between document and its anchor" is "an encoding of a certain politics into the technology itself"; it was not designed "to limit agency", but "the asymmetry created by the one-way link would encourage conditions where technologies that seek to uncover user data to track, profile and sell would emerge." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Author's own definition of legibility: "I use the term legibility to mean whether a user can see and understand what a web technology is doing, who controls it and what it does with their behavior." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Method: "affordance analysis" borrowed from UX/HCI "as a way to move past the question of intent and close-read specific web technologies". Each artefact is asked four questions: "What does it look like it does? ... What does it actually do? ... What does it hide? ... Whose interest does it serve?" The evidence is hands-on: view-source and the JavaScript console on restored Geocities pages, Facebook and YouTube — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Why affordances and not resistance: "the question is not about whether a user can resist the taking away of agency, but what those technologies make possible or don't allow for in the first place." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Structure: three pairs under three headings. "Traversal: The Webring vs. the Algorithmic Feed"; "Measurement: The Hit-Counter vs. the Like Button"; "Memory: The Stable URL vs. Opaque Platform Links" — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Traversal: a broken Geocities webring is still readable in its source ("Its mechanism is legible even in failure" is the caption used for the hit counter; for the webring: "Even though the mechanism of the webring was broken, I was still able to see exactly how it would have worked. The Facebook feed, though, is working and invisible."). Facebook's class names are "labeled in ways that make their meaning opaque to me". The feed is "not agency-giving based on its lack of legibility." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- EdgeRank as a case of a formula that grew unreadable: three public factors in 2010 (affinity, edge weight, time decay) versus "as many as 100,000 individual weights" later. "My argument here of Facebook's algorithm being an illegible and opaque technology in its structure" — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Measurement: the hit counter "hides nothing ... There is no gap between what it appears to do, and what it actually does ... It is dumb by design." The Like button is "a data collection instrument presented as an expression mechanism" and "measurement infrastructure that pretends to be legible, but has a hidden motive, transferring power from the author/reader relationship to the platform." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Memory: a Geocities URL "acts like an address"; a YouTube URL uses "an opaque database key ... controlled entirely by the platform", which "can be revoked at any moment", so "power is moved away from the public record to the platform." A tracking call to doubleclick.net is "invisible to the user unless they inspect the console directly, an example of Gaver's hidden affordance." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)
- Conclusion: "technology that centers the user makes its workings legible, while technology that centers the platforms keeps them hidden." and "liberation on the web is not a question of ownership, or regulation but rather a question of legibility. A user who cannot read what a technology is doing cannot meaningfully consent to it." — [The Legible Web](https://pandji.co/writing/the-legible-web)

### Inferences
- The published vocabulary is about hiding (opaque, hidden, invisible) more than about compression. "Flattening" names a different operation (many values reduced to one) from "hiding" (a mechanism placed out of view). The thesis will need to connect the two: an index hides by summarising.
- The author uses "legibility" from the user's side (can I read the system?). In James C. Scott's use, legibility runs the other way (the state making people readable). The author does not cite Scott, and the two senses pull in opposite directions; this is worth stating in the thesis.
- "Agency" is tied to understanding and consent, not to control or customisation: you have agency when you can read what the thing does.
- Heidegger's "revealing" is already in the author's toolkit and matches the thesis phrase "choose what to hide and what to reveal".

### Gaps
- No published definition of "flattening" or "index/indexing" by the author.
- No published use of "threshold" in the personal-threshold sense.
