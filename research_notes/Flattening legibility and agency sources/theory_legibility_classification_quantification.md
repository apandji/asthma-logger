# Theory of legibility, classification, indexing, quantification and commensuration: annotated bibliography

**How to read the verification marks**

- **[V]** = bibliographic details (and DOI/URL where given) confirmed this session on a publisher, journal, DOI, library or author page.
- **[V-quote]** = the quoted words were read this session on the primary source (or its abstract).
- **[2nd-quote]** = the quote was confirmed only through a secondary source that attributes it to the work; check the book before putting it on a slide.
- **[memory]** = the item is canonical and I am confident of it, but it was **not** confirmed by a search this session. Treat as *unverified* until checked.
- No page numbers are given unless a source supplied them. Where none is given, do not add one without the book in hand.

Thesis shorthand used below: **T** = "indexing flattens, chooses what to hide/reveal, and takes agency away"; **R1** = "the problem is a summary that cannot be opened back up"; **R2** = "the problem is replacing a person's own number with a population's number".

## 1. Legibility and state simplification (Scott): what he claims and who critiques him

### Takeaway
Scott's claim is narrower than it is usually quoted: simplification is a normal and often beneficial tool of statecraft, and disaster needs four things together (legibility + high-modernist ideology + authoritarian state + a prostrate civil society). The best critiques (Li, Ferguson, Herzfeld) say he over-centres "the state", under-describes what schemes actually *do*, and romanticises local knowledge. This helps the thesis: the refined position (R1/R2) is more defensible than a blanket "flattening is bad".

### Cited Findings

**1. Scott, James C. (1998). *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed*. New Haven: Yale University Press.** [V: Yale UP, hardcover March 1998, paperback 1999]
- Core claim: states must make society "legible" (standard measures, cadastral maps, permanent surnames) in order to tax, conscript and administer; these simplifications are partial by design and, when imposed as if they were the whole, destroy the practical local knowledge (*mētis*) that made things work. Failure requires four elements combined: administrative ordering, high-modernist ideology, an authoritarian state and a civil society too weak to resist. — [review summary](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/seeing-like-a-state-james-c-scott); [Sunstein review, *The New Republic* 1998](https://home.uchicago.edu/csunstei/moreisless.html)
- "Thin simplifications": this exact phrase was not found in search snippets, but it is the title of Scott's chapter 9, "Thin Simplifications and Practical Knowledge: Mētis" **[memory — check the table of contents]**. Scott's more frequent term is "state simplifications".
- Quote **[memory, unverified wording and page]**: "Legibility is a condition of manipulation." Verify in the book before use.
- Scott's own concession **[memory, unverified wording]**: in the Introduction he says these state simplifications are as vital to welfare and freedom as to the designs of a modern despot, and he names public-health uses (vaccination, sanitation, epidemic control) as things legibility enables. Sunstein's review confirms the general point: efforts to make society transparent to the state are not wrong in themselves but create risks — [Sunstein](https://home.uchicago.edu/csunstei/moreisless.html).
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** (simplification selects what counts) but **complicates** it: Scott locates the harm in *imposition without recourse*, not in the map itself, which is close to R1.

**2. Li, Tania Murray (2005). "Beyond 'the State' and Failed Schemes." *American Anthropologist* 107(3): 383-394. DOI: 10.1525/aa.2005.107.3.383.** [V] — [author page](https://www.taniali.org/papers/beyond-the-state-and-failed-schemes)
- Core claim: five moves beyond Scott. There is no single all-seeing state "up there"; many parties (reformers, scientists, NGOs) try to govern; the frame should be "improvement", not only high modernism; and Scott's question should be replaced by Ferguson's.
- Quote [V-quote, abstract]: she proposes to "reframe the question posed by Scott—why have certain schemes designed to improve the human condition failed?—to examine the question posed so provocatively by James Ferguson: What do these schemes do?"
- Relation to thesis: **Complicates**. A design audience can use Li to ask the author: stop asking whether the index "fails"; ask what it *does* and for whom. Also warns that "the system" doing the flattening is rarely one actor.

**3. Ferguson, James (2005). "Seeing Like an Oil Company: Space, Security, and Global Capital in Neoliberal Africa." *American Anthropologist* 107(3): 377-382.** [V for existence, journal and issue via search snippet; page range and DOI 10.1525/aa.2005.107.3.377 are **[memory]**]
- Core claim: Scott's model of a homogenising, grid-making state does not describe global capital, which works through enclaves and deliberately leaves most territory *illegible*. — [search summary from the same AA issue](https://www.taniali.org/papers/beyond-the-state-and-failed-schemes)
- Relation to thesis: **Complicates**: power today may operate by *selective* legibility, not uniform flattening.

**4. Herzfeld, Michael (2005). "Political Optics and the Occlusion of Intimate Knowledge." *American Anthropologist* 107(3): 369-376.** [V for existence via University of Melbourne record; pages **[memory]**] — [record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1125484-political-optics-and-the-occlusion-of-intimate-knowledge)
- Core claim: Scott overgeneralises "resistance" and underestimates bureaucrats' complicity with local populations; officials use *mētis* too.
- Relation to thesis: **Opposes** a clean system-versus-person split.

**5. Sunstein, Cass R. (1998). "More Is Less" (review of Scott). *The New Republic*.** [V] — [text](https://home.uchicago.edu/csunstei/moreisless.html)
- Core claim: sympathetic but notes legibility is not inherently wrong; it creates risks.
- Relation to thesis: **Counter-weight** from a mainstream legal scholar.

### Inferences
- The strongest defensible reading of Scott for this thesis: the damage comes when the simplified record *replaces* the thing and cannot be corrected from below. That is R1 in Scott's vocabulary.
- Popular critiques (blog reviews) that Scott ignores successes such as the Green Revolution exist but are not scholarly sources; cite Li/Ferguson/Herzfeld instead — [example of the popular critique](https://progressforum.org/posts/fAFnwpA2xQnKvtHab/a-review-of-seeing-like-a-state-in-six-tweets).

### Gaps
- Exact Scott quotations and page numbers were not verified against the book text; the Yale UP product page was not retrieved.
- Brad DeLong's Hayekian critique ("Forests, Trees, and Intellectual Roots") is often cited but was not verified this session.

## 2. Classification, standards, infrastructure and invisible work

### Takeaway
Bowker and Star give the thesis its most precise vocabulary: classifications are invisible when they work, every scheme has residual categories ("other", "misc."), and "torque" names the twisting of a life that does not fit its category. They also insist classification is unavoidable and often good, so they support R1/R2 better than a blanket critique.

### Cited Findings

**6. Bowker, Geoffrey C. & Star, Susan Leigh (1999). *Sorting Things Out: Classification and Its Consequences*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (Inside Technology).** [V] — [MIT Press Bookstore](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262522953); [authors' excerpt page](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gbowker/classification)
- Core claim: classification systems (ICD, nursing interventions, apartheid race categories) are infrastructure that does moral and political work while staying out of sight. Each category valorises one point of view and silences another. "Torque" is what happens when a person's biography and the classification pull in different directions or at different rates; "residual categories" hold whatever the scheme cannot represent.
- Quote: the phrase "To classify is human" is the book's well-known opening line **[memory — not found in search snippets; verify]**. The torque description ("trajectories that may pull or torque each other over time") was confirmed in summary form only — [Tufts-hosted chapter PDF](https://sites.tufts.edu/classification/files/2020/09/Bowker-Star-apartheid.pdf).
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T and R2 directly**: torque is the harm of being read through a category built for a population. **Complicates**: the authors say the answer is not to stop classifying but to keep the classification's making visible and revisable (R1).

**7. Star, Susan Leigh (1999). "The Ethnography of Infrastructure." *American Behavioral Scientist* 43(3): 377-391. DOI: 10.1177/00027649921955326.** [V] — [DOI record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/the-ethnography-of-infrastructure/)
- Core claim: infrastructure is relational, embedded, learned as part of membership, and "becomes visible upon breakdown" **[memory for the phrase]**. Studying "boring things" (lists, codes, forms) reveals whose work and whose categories were built in.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports**: gives a method (read the index as infrastructure; look for the master narrative and the invisible work).

**8. Star, Susan Leigh & Strauss, Anselm (1999). "Layers of Silence, Arenas of Voice: The Ecology of Visible and Invisible Work." *Computer Supported Cooperative Work* 8: 9-30. DOI: 10.1023/A:1008651105359.** [V] — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1023/A:1008651105359.pdf)
- Core claim: no work is inherently visible or invisible; visibility is produced by indicators and by who defines what counts. Making work visible is double-edged: it brings recognition but also surveillance and reification.
- Relation to thesis: **Complicates**: revealing more is not automatically empowering. Useful when someone says "so just show everything".

**9. Lampland, Martha & Star, Susan Leigh (eds.) (2009). *Standards and Their Stories: How Quantifying, Classifying, and Formalizing Practices Shape Everyday Life*. Ithaca: Cornell University Press. ISBN 978-0-8014-7461-3.** [V] — [library record](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00003441268/Description)
- Core claim: standards are nested, unevenly distributed, relative to communities of practice, and codify ethics and values; someone always bears the cost of not fitting.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T**; gives everyday examples a design audience will recognise.

**10. Suchman, Lucy (1994). "Do Categories Have Politics? The Language/Action Perspective Reconsidered." *Computer Supported Cooperative Work* 2(3): 177-190. DOI: 10.1007/BF00749015.** [V; some sources date it 1993] — [Lancaster record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/27036)
- Core claim: building a fixed category scheme for communicative acts into software is a form of discipline over users; categories in systems carry an agenda of control. Terry Winograd replied in the same journal (DOI 10.1007/BF00749016), defending explicit categories as useful for coordination.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** from inside design/HCI, and comes with a ready-made **counter** (Winograd).

### Inferences
- "Residual category" is a concrete design test: what does the index do with what it cannot bin? (In the author's own project terms: is a missing value shown as missing, or silently as "no"?)

### Gaps
- Exact wording of Bowker & Star's definitions of torque and residual categories was not read from the book itself.

## 3. Quantification and commensuration

### Takeaway
The sociology of quantification says numbers do not just describe; they commensurate (make different things comparable by discarding what differs), travel, and change what they measure (reactivity, looping). Porter adds the key complication: numbers win because they let strangers trust each other without personal knowledge. That is both the benefit and the mechanism of the loss.

### Cited Findings

**11. Espeland, Wendy Nelson & Stevens, Mitchell L. (1998). "Commensuration as a Social Process." *Annual Review of Sociology* 24: 313-343. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.soc.24.1.313.** [V] — [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev.soc.24.1.313)
- Core claim: commensuration is the comparison of different entities by a common metric. It reduces and simplifies information, turns qualities into quantities, and is a form of power; calling something "incommensurable" is itself a political claim.
- Quote [V-quote, abstract]: "Although it is evident in routine decision-making and a crucial vehicle of rationalization, commensuration as a general social process has been given little consideration by sociologists."
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T**: this is the most exact scholarly name for "flattening". Recommend the author define flattening *as* commensuration plus loss of the path back.

**12. Espeland, Wendy Nelson & Stevens, Mitchell L. (2008). "A Sociology of Quantification." *European Journal of Sociology* 49(3): 401-436. DOI: 10.1017/S0003975609000150.** [V; some CVs date it 2009] — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0003975609000150)
- Core claim: distinguishes numbers that *mark* (name, identify) from numbers that *commensurate* (value, rank). Proposes five dimensions for study: the work quantification needs, its reactivity, its tendency to discipline, its authority, and its aesthetics; ends with a call for an "ethics of numbers".
- Relation to thesis: **Supports and sharpens**: the marking/commensurating split maps onto "a reading with units" versus "a score".

**13. Espeland, Wendy Nelson & Sauder, Michael (2007). "Rankings and Reactivity: How Public Measures Recreate Social Worlds." *American Journal of Sociology* 113(1): 1-40. DOI: 10.1086/517897.** [V] — [University of Iowa record](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rankings-and-Reactivity-How-Public-Measures/9984002327102771)
- Core claim: law-school rankings change the schools they rank through two mechanisms, self-fulfilling prophecy and commensuration; people redistribute effort toward what is counted.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports the agency claim** with empirical evidence: the measured reorganise themselves around the measure.

**14. Porter, Theodore M. (1995). *Trust in Numbers: The Pursuit of Objectivity in Science and Public Life*. Princeton: Princeton University Press (new edition with new preface, 2020).** [V] — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/77331)
- Core claim: quantification spreads not where experts are strong but where they are weak and distrusted; rules and numbers ("mechanical objectivity") substitute for personal judgement that outsiders will not accept. Numbers are a means of communicating beyond the bounds of locality and community.
- Quote: "quantification is a technology of distance" — widely attributed to the preface **[memory; this session confirmed only the secondary form "technologies of distance"** via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_in_Numbers) **— verify in the book]**.
- Relation to thesis: **Both**. Supports: distance is exactly what strips local context. **Opposes**: distance is also what lets a patient and a stranger-clinician, or a citizen and an agency, hold each other to account without having to trust the person.

**15. Desrosières, Alain (1998). *The Politics of Large Numbers: A History of Statistical Reasoning*. Trans. Camille Naish. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press (French original 1993).** [V] — [Wikipedia entry with edition details](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Politics_of_Large_Numbers)
- Core claim: statistics and the state were built together. Before counting comes "making things that hold": conventions of equivalence that decide which different cases will be treated as the same **[concept names from memory]**.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T**: flattening happens at the convention of equivalence, before any number exists.

**16. Hacking, Ian (2006). "Making Up People." *London Review of Books* 28(16), 17 August 2006.** [V] — [LRB](https://lrb.co.uk/v28/n16/ian-hacking/making-up-people)
- Core claim: classifying people changes them; the classified respond, so the target moves. Hacking lists "engines of discovery" that begin "1. Count! 2. Quantify! 3. Create Norms! 4. Correlate!".
- Quote [V-quote]: "Sometimes, our sciences create kinds of people that in a certain sense did not exist before. I call this 'making up people'." And: "The target has moved. I call this the 'looping effect'."
- Relation to thesis: **Complicates the agency claim in a useful way**: classification removes some agency but people also take categories up and use them; agency loops.

**17. Hacking, Ian (1982). "Biopower and the Avalanche of Printed Numbers." *Humanities in Society* 5: 279-295; developed in *The Taming of Chance* (Cambridge University Press, 1990).** [V for title, journal and year; the page range comes from a mailing-list citation, treat as **unverified**; the 1990 book is **[memory]**] — [article PDF](https://moodle.brynmawr.edu/pluginfile.php/24320/mod_resource/content/1/Hacking.%20Biopower%20and%20the%20Avalanche%20of%20Printed%20Numbers.pdf)
- Core claim: between about 1820 and 1840 European states began printing numbers about populations at a huge scale; new categories of person had to be invented to hold the counts; "normal" became a statistical idea.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports R2**: the history of how the population's number became the standard for the individual.

**18. Merry, Sally Engle (2016). *The Seductions of Quantification: Measuring Human Rights, Gender Violence, and Sex Trafficking*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.** [V; one source gives a different subtitle, the one here matches the Chicago listing] — [Chicago course intro PDF](https://chicagoreference.com/dam/ucp/books/pdf/course_intro/978-0-226-26128-7_course_intro.pdf)
- Core claim: indicators take contested, locally specific information, quantify and package it, and present it as objective. Merry names "expertise inertia" and "data inertia": what gets measured is what experts already know how to measure and what data already exists **[term names from memory]**.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** ("chooses what to hide" becomes: what was cheap to count).

**19. Muller, Jerry Z. (2018). *The Tyranny of Metrics*. Princeton: Princeton University Press.** [V] — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/65195)
- Core claim: "metric fixation" is the belief that standardised measures can replace judgement, that publishing them ensures accountability, and that rewards should be tied to them. Muller is explicit that measurement is useful when it informs judgement and harmful when it replaces it.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports**, in accessible prose; **complicates**: not anti-measurement.

**20. Mau, Steffen (2019). *The Metric Society: On the Quantification of the Social*. Trans. Sharon Howe. Cambridge: Polity (German 2017).** [V] — [Wiley/Polity](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/humanities-social-sciences/sociology-12so/political-sociology-12so2/the-metric-society-978-1-5095-3041-0)
- Core claim: ratings, rankings and scores create a general regime of comparison that turns into competition and new status inequalities.
- Counter [V]: Angèle Christin's review in the *European Journal of Sociology* says Mau relies on ominous predictions and future-tense claims and grants metrics too much power — [review](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-sociology-archives-europeennes-de-sociologie/article/abs/powerful-metrics-steffen-mau-the-metric-society-on-the-quantification-of-the-social-cambridge-uk-polity-2019-200-p/DD52661A32E870E16AA9F6DF725DBB19)
- Relation to thesis: **Supports**, but cite with Christin's caution: claims about what metrics do to people need evidence.

**21. Campbell, Donald T. (1979). "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change." *Evaluation and Program Planning* 2(1): 67-90. DOI: 10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X (first issued 1976 as an occasional paper).** [V] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:epplan:v:2:y:1979:i:1:p:67-90)
- Core claim (Campbell's law) [2nd-quote, as quoted in Muller's book excerpt]: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."
- Goodhart's law: the popular wording "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" is Marilyn Strathern's 1997 restatement ("'Improving Ratings': Audit in the British University System", *European Review* 5(3): 305-321), not Goodhart's own 1975 text **[memory — unverified this session]**.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T**; note the mechanism is *use for decisions*, not summarising as such, which fits the refinement.

**22. Fourcade, Marion & Healy, Kieran (2017). "Seeing Like a Market." *Socio-Economic Review* 15(1): 9-29. DOI: 10.1093/ser/mww033.** [V] — [author page](https://kieranhealy.org/publications/slam/). Also *The Ordinal Society* (Harvard University Press, 2024) [V via Berkeley faculty page only].
- Core claim: firms now do what Scott's states did, but with individual behavioural data: people are scored and sorted, and the scores become a form of capital that shapes life chances.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports and updates** Scott for platforms; **complicates R2**: these scores are highly individual, yet still disempowering, so "your own number" is not automatically safe.

**23. Nguyen, C. Thi (2024). "Value Capture." *Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy* 27(3): 469-504. DOI: 10.26556/jesp.v27i3.3048.** [V] — [journal](https://jesp.org/index.php/jesp/article/view/3048/494)
- Core claim: value capture happens when a person's rich, subtle values are replaced by a simplified, usually quantified version supplied by an institution or technology (steps, GPA, likes). The harm is to autonomy: you stop tailoring your values to your own life.
- Relation to thesis: **Strongest recent philosophical support for the agency claim and for R2** (an outside metric standing in for one's own).

### Inferences
- Across this literature the loss is located at three moments: (a) the convention of equivalence (Desrosières), (b) the commensurating step (Espeland & Stevens), (c) use for decisions and rewards (Campbell, Espeland & Sauder, Muller). The thesis should say which moment its design intervenes in.

### Gaps
- Porter's exact "technology of distance" sentence and its page were not confirmed on a primary text.
- Goodhart (1975) and Strathern (1997) not verified this session.

## 4. Indexes, indicators, black boxes and immutable mobiles

### Takeaway
The indicator literature says outright that a composite index hides its theory, its weights and its missing data behind one number, and that the simplification is what gives it authority. Latour supplies two tools: "black-boxing" (the hidden interior) and, more useful for R1, "circulating reference" (a good abstraction keeps a chain you can walk back along).

### Cited Findings

**24. Davis, Kevin E., Kingsbury, Benedict & Merry, Sally Engle (2012). "Indicators as a Technology of Global Governance." *Law & Society Review* 46(1): 71-104. DOI: 10.1111/j.1540-5893.2012.00473.x.** [V] — [IILJ](https://www.iilj.org/publications/indicators-technology-global-governance/); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1583431)
- Core claim: an indicator is a named collection of rank-ordered data that simplifies raw data about a complex social phenomenon so that units can be compared. Indicators carry an embedded theory of what is good, and shift power toward those who produce them. Case studies: World Bank Doing Business, UN Human Development Index.
- Quote [V-quote, abstract]: "The use of indicators is a prominent feature of contemporary global governance."
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** for indexes specifically; the standard citation for "an index is governance".

**25. Rottenburg, Richard, Merry, Sally Engle, Park, Sung-Joon & Mugler, Johanna (eds.) (2015). *The World of Indicators: The Making of Governmental Knowledge through Quantification*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.** [V for editors, publisher, year; DOI 10.1017/CBO9781316091265 is inferred from the Cambridge identifier, **unverified**] — [Cambridge Core chapter](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/books/world-of-indicators/retroaction-how-indicators-feed-back-onto-quantified-actors/5368643D55B70177B06B3520A6C66828)
- Core claim: ethnographic cases of how indicators are made and how they "retroact" on those measured (the volume includes a chapter by Desrosières on retroaction).
- Relation to thesis: **Supports**, with fieldwork rather than assertion.

**26. OECD & European Commission Joint Research Centre (2008). *Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators: Methodology and User Guide*. Paris: OECD Publishing. DOI: 10.1787/9789264043466-en.** [V] — [JRC full text](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/jrc47008_handbook_final.pdf)
- Core claim: the makers' own manual. It lists pros and cons of composites (they summarise multidimensional reality and aid communication; they can mislead if poorly built, and weighting and aggregation choices are contestable) and requires that a composite can be decomposed back into its parts ("back to the detail") and tested by sensitivity analysis **[step names from memory; the pros/cons table wording was not retrieved this session]**.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports R1 from the opposing camp**: the statisticians who build indexes already say an index must open back up. Strong to cite, because it shows R1 is a professional norm that is often not met, not an outsider's complaint.

**27. Latour, Bruno (1987). *Science in Action: How to Follow Scientists and Engineers through Society*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press; and Latour (1999). *Pandora's Hope: Essays on the Reality of Science Studies*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.** [**memory** for publisher/year of both; the blackboxing definition is attributed to *Pandora's Hope* p. 304 by secondary sources]
- Core claim (black-boxing) [2nd-quote]: "the way scientific and technical work is made invisible by its own success"; when a machine runs well one attends only to inputs and outputs. — [Wikipedia, citing Latour 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackboxing)
- Core claim (circulating reference, *Pandora's Hope* ch. 2) **[memory — a search this session did not retrieve it]**: in following soil scientists in the Amazon, Latour argues that each step from forest to diagram loses locality and particularity and gains compatibility and standardisation, and that reference holds only if the chain of transformations stays traceable in both directions.
- Relation to thesis: **Black-boxing supports T. Circulating reference is the best theoretical ground for R1**: abstraction is legitimate when the chain is reversible. The author should read chapter 2 and quote it directly.

**28. Latour, Bruno (1986). "Visualisation and Cognition: Thinking with Eyes and Hands." *Knowledge and Society* 6: 1-40. Reprinted as "Drawing Things Together" in Lynch & Woolgar (eds.), *Representation in Scientific Activity* (MIT Press, 1990), pp. 19-68.** [V] — [Latour's site](https://www.bruno-latour.fr/node/293.html)
- Core claim: power in science and administration comes from inscriptions that are mobile, immutable, flat, scalable and combinable ("immutable mobiles"); whoever gathers them in one place can dominate at a distance. Latour names flatness as a property that makes inscriptions masterable **[the list of properties is from memory]**.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports and complicates**: Latour describes flattening as the source of knowledge's power, without condemning it. A scholarly origin for the word "flat" in STS.

### Inferences
- The combination of 26 and 27 gives R1 a two-sided defence: STS theory (reference must circulate back) and statistical practice (composites must decompose).

### Gaps
- Latour quotations need checking against the books; no primary text was retrieved.
- No source was found this session that empirically measures how often published indexes actually expose their weights.

## 5. Data as constructed

### Takeaway
There is wide agreement that data are made, not found. This cuts both ways for the thesis: it supports "the index chooses", but it also undermines any appeal to opening a summary back up into "raw" truth. What you open it into is another construction.

### Cited Findings

**29. Drucker, Johanna (2011). "Humanities Approaches to Graphical Display." *Digital Humanities Quarterly* 5(1).** [V] — [DHQ](https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/5/1/000091/000091.html)
- Core claim: visualisation tools borrowed from the sciences assume observer-independent certainty; humanists should treat data as *capta* and design graphics that show ambiguity and interpretation.
- Quote [V-quote]: "Capta is 'taken' actively while data is assumed to be a 'given' able to be recorded and observed." And: "Data are capta, taken not given, constructed as an interpretation of the phenomenal world, not inherent in it."
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T**, and is the key design-facing source. **Complicates R1**: the thing a summary opens into is itself taken.

**30. Gitelman, Lisa (ed.) (2013). *"Raw Data" Is an Oxymoron*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (Infrastructures series).** [V] — [library record](https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00004602447/Description); [introduction PDF](https://dsl.lsu.edu/nehtextualdata/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/RawData.pdf)
- Core claim: data are always already "cooked": imagined, collected and formatted for a purpose. The title comes from Bowker, *Memory Practices in the Sciences* (MIT Press, 2005).
- Quote [V-quote, the volume's epigraph]: "'Raw data' is both an oxymoron and a bad idea." — Geoffrey C. Bowker.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T; complicates R1** for the same reason as Drucker.

**31. Daston, Lorraine & Galison, Peter (2007). *Objectivity*. New York: Zone Books. ISBN 978-1-890951-78-8.** [V] — [*Medical History* review, Cambridge](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9B7FFAB3FDDC977145827CCDF2962F91/S0025727300003896a.pdf/lorraine-daston-and-peter-galison-objectivity-new-york-zone-books-2007-pp-501-32-colour-plates-108-black-and-white-illus-pound2595-dollar3895-hardback-978-1-890951-78-8.pdf)
- Core claim: objectivity has a history. Scientific atlases moved from "truth-to-nature" (the idealised type) to "mechanical objectivity" (around 1850: suppress the observer) to "trained judgment" (around 1920: the expert interprets).
- Relation to thesis: **Complicates**: the urge to remove human judgement from a summary is one historical virtue among several; "trained judgment" is a respectable precedent for designs that keep a person in the reading.

### Inferences
- A critic can use 29-31 to say: "there is no un-flattened original to go back to". The answer is that R1 asks for traceability to a *less* processed layer and its conditions of capture, not to raw truth.

### Gaps
- Bowker (2005) *Memory Practices in the Sciences* was confirmed only as the source of the epigraph, not separately.

## 6. "Flattening" as a term: who uses it and how

### Takeaway
"Flattening" is not a settled technical term in STS. It is used in at least three different senses: homogenisation of culture (Chayka), the abstract homogeneous space of perspective (Steyerl), and the flat inscription that makes the world masterable (Latour). The thesis must define its own sense; the closest rigorous synonyms are commensuration and black-boxing.

### Cited Findings

**32. Chayka, Kyle (2024). *Filterworld: How Algorithms Flattened Culture*. New York: Doubleday.** [V] — [Hyperallergic review](https://hyperallergic.com/864904/can-we-free-ourselves-from-algorithms-filterworld-kyle-chayka/)
- Core claim: recommendation systems make culture homogeneous and frictionless; "Filterworld" is his name for the network of algorithmic recommendation. "Flattening" here means sameness: the least ambiguous, least disruptive content gets promoted **[paraphrase; his exact definition sentence was not retrieved]**.
- Counter [V]: reviewers (Anna Shechtman in *The Yale Review*; Kevin Munger) argue the thesis is asserted more than shown and that his own examples cut against "iron-clad" rules — [Yale Review](https://yalereview.org/article/anna-shechtman-algorithm-kyle-chayka-taylor-lorenz); [Munger](https://kevinmunger.substack.com/p/the-algorithm-is-the-only-critique)
- Relation to thesis: **Supports in spirit, but is journalism, and uses flattening to mean homogenisation, not loss of the path back.** Cite as evidence the word is in circulation, not as theory.

**33. Steyerl, Hito (2011). "In Free Fall: A Thought Experiment on Vertical Perspective." *e-flux journal* 24 (April 2011). Reprinted in *The Wretched of the Screen* (Sternberg Press, 2012).** [V] — [e-flux](https://www.e-flux.com/journal/24/67860/in-free-fall-a-thought-experiment-on-vertical-perspective/)
- Core claim: linear perspective, and now the vertical view from drones and satellites, are constructed ways of seeing that position a viewer and serve domination; they are not how anyone perceives.
- Quote [V-quote]: "Instead, it computes a mathematical, flattened, infinite, continuous, and homogenous space, and declares it to be reality." And: "Thus, linear perspective is based on an abstraction, and does not correspond to any subjective perception."
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** with a verified use of "flattened" in art/media theory, tied to the view from above (links to Scott).

**34. Golumbia, David (2009). *The Cultural Logic of Computation*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.** [V] — [Monoskop](https://monoskop.org/log/?p=1978); [review, Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/403304)
- Core claim: "computationalism" is the belief that mind and society are fundamentally computational; it favours hierarchy, standardisation and central control. Golumbia uses Deleuze and Guattari's "striated" (gridded) versus "smooth" space **[memory — the search did not confirm the term]**; he does not, as far as verified, define "flattening".
- Counter [V]: reviewers find the link to right-wing politics forced and the treatment of rationalism reductive — [PopMatters](https://popmatters.com/94465-the-cultural-logic-of-computation-by-david-golumbia-2496031375.html)
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** on agency and centralisation, but his vocabulary is striation, which is closer to gridding than flattening.

- Latour (entry 28) is the earliest strong use: flat inscriptions are what can be dominated. — [Latour's site](https://www.bruno-latour.fr/node/293.html)
- One summary of Scott found this session describes legibility as "flattening local arrangements into standard, countable forms", but that is a summariser's wording, not Scott's. — [summary](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/scott-1998.md)

### Inferences
- A defensible definition for the thesis: *flattening = commensuration (Espeland & Stevens) that has been black-boxed (Latour), so the reference no longer circulates back.* That uses established terms and matches R1.

### Gaps
- **Benjamin Bratton**: *The Stack: On Software and Sovereignty* (MIT Press, 2015) exists **[memory]**, but I found no evidence this session that he defines "flattening"; do not attribute the term to him without checking.
- "Flat ontology" (DeLanda, Bryant) is a different, positive use of "flat" in philosophy and may confuse the audience; not researched here.
- "Context collapse" (Marwick & boyd, 2011) is a related media-studies term; not verified this session.

## 7. Agency: simplification as loss of agency, or as its condition

### Takeaway
Illich, Winner and Suchman tie agency to whether a tool can be understood, adapted and used on the person's own terms, not to whether it simplifies. Sen is the important complication: he both criticises single-number measures and helped create one (the HDI).

### Cited Findings

**35. Illich, Ivan (1973). *Tools for Conviviality*. New York: Harper & Row (UK: Calder & Boyars).** [V] — [Wikipedia, edition details](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tools_for_Conviviality)
- Core claim: past a threshold, industrial tools and expert institutions make people dependent; "convivial" tools are responsibly limited ones that people can use autonomously for purposes they choose.
- Quote [2nd-quote; wording from reprints and secondary sources, verify in the book]: conviviality as "autonomous and creative intercourse among persons, and the intercourse of persons with their environment".
- Relation to thesis: **Supports the agency claim**; gives a design criterion (can the person use the summary for their own ends without an expert?).

**36. Winner, Langdon (1980). "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" *Daedalus* 109(1): 121-136. Stable URL: jstor.org/stable/20024652.** [V] — [American Academy](https://www.amacad.org/publication/daedalus/artifacts-politics)
- Core claim: technical arrangements can embody forms of power, either by design in a particular setting or because some technologies require particular social arrangements.
- Counter **[memory — unverified]**: Joerges, Bernward (1999), "Do Politics Have Artefacts?", *Social Studies of Science* 29(3), showed the Moses low-bridges example is historically doubtful.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T** (design choices are political). If the author uses the bridges story, expect the Joerges objection.

**37. Suchman, Lucy (1987). *Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human-Machine Communication*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press (2nd ed. *Human-Machine Reconfigurations*, 2007).** [**memory** — not verified this session]
- Core claim: plans are resources for action, not its cause; a system that models the user through a fixed plan cannot see the situated work people do to make sense, so breakdowns are invisible to it.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports the agency claim**: abstractions of a person's activity miss the situated part. See also entry 10 [V].

**38. Sen, Amartya (1999). *Development as Freedom*. New York: Knopf.** [**memory** for publisher; the capability approach is verified via the Stanford Encyclopedia] — [SEP, "The Capability Approach"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)
- Core claim: wellbeing should be judged by what people are actually able to do and be, not by resources or utility; evaluative frameworks differ in their "informational basis", i.e. what they let in and what they exclude. The same resources convert into different capabilities for different people.
- Relation to thesis: **Supports T and R2** in rigorous form ("informational basis" = what an index reveals and hides; conversion factors = why a population figure misdescribes a person). **Complicates**: Sen co-developed the Human Development Index as a deliberately crude single number to compete with GDP for attention **[memory — his stated reservations and reasons should be cited from the UNDP Human Development Report 1999 special contribution; not verified this session]**.

**39. Molenaar, Peter C. M. (2004). "A Manifesto on Psychology as Idiographic Science: Bringing the Person Back into Scientific Psychology, This Time Forever." *Measurement: Interdisciplinary Research and Perspectives* 2(4): 201-218. DOI: 10.1207/s15366359mea0204_1.** [V] — [citing article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12124-007-9011-1)
- Core claim: results about variation *between* people apply to an individual's variation *over time* only under ergodicity (stationarity and the same model for everyone), which human processes rarely satisfy.
- Popularised in Rose, Todd (2016), *The End of Average* [V for author, title, year; publisher HarperOne is **unverified**] — [review](https://greaterwrong.com/posts/XsdcDiJEnAscrwoE2/book-review-the-end-of-average)
- Relation to thesis: **The most direct scientific support for R2.** It is a formal argument, not a rhetorical one, for why a population's number cannot stand in for a person's.

### Inferences
- These sources converge on a criterion compatible with the refinement: agency depends on (a) whether the person can inspect and contest the abstraction (Illich, Suchman; R1) and (b) whether it is about them or about a population (Sen, Molenaar; R2).

### Gaps
- Canguilhem, *The Normal and the Pathological* (the norm as individual versus statistical) would strengthen R2 but was not researched.
- Sen's own statements on the HDI were not retrieved.

## 8. Counter-arguments the author will face

### Takeaway
Five serious objections: (1) attention is scarce, so compression is a service; (2) numbers build trust between strangers and protect against arbitrary judgement; (3) legibility delivers public health; (4) plain, simplified risk formats demonstrably help patients; (5) "opening it up" (transparency) does not by itself give understanding or power. The refinement (R1, R2) survives all five better than the broad thesis does.

### Cited Findings

**40. Simon, Herbert A. (1971). "Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World." In M. Greenberger (ed.), *Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest*, pp. 37-52. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins Press.** [V] — [full text PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/design/1971-simon.pdf)
- Core claim: information consumes attention, so an information-processing system helps only if it absorbs more than it produces: it should listen and think more than it speaks.
- Quote [V-quote]: "Hence a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention and a need to allocate that attention efficiently among the overabundance of information sources that might consume it."
- Simon (1955), "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 69(1): 99-118 **[memory — not verified this session]** is the source for bounded rationality.
- Relation to thesis: **Opposes T**: un-flattened information can reduce agency by exhausting attention. Compatible with R1 (summary first, detail on demand).

**41. Gigerenzer, Gerd, Gaissmaier, Wolfgang, Kurz-Milcke, Elke, Schwartz, Lisa M. & Woloshin, Steven (2007). "Helping Doctors and Patients Make Sense of Health Statistics." *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 8(2): 53-96. DOI: 10.1111/j.1539-6053.2008.00033.x.** [V] — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26161749/)
- Core claim: doctors and patients widely misread health statistics, and the cause is largely presentation. Recommended: frequency statements over single-event probabilities, absolute over relative risks, mortality rates over survival rates, natural frequencies over conditional probabilities.
- Relation to thesis: **Opposes T and partly supports R1**: the right simplification *increases* a patient's ability to decide. Natural frequencies work because they keep the reference class visible, which is a small case of a summary that can be opened. Also note for R2: risk communication is, necessarily, a population number offered to a person.

- **Porter (entry 14)**: impersonal numbers constrain the arbitrary power of experts and let outsiders check insiders. Removing the number can return power to whoever holds discretion. — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/77331)
- **Scott (entry 1) and Sunstein (entry 5)**: legibility is not inherently wrong and underlies welfare functions. — [Sunstein](https://home.uchicago.edu/csunstei/moreisless.html)
- **OECD/JRC (entry 26)**: composite indicators are defended as tools for summarising complex realities and for public communication, on condition they are transparent and decomposable. — [Handbook](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/jrc47008_handbook_final.pdf)
- **Winograd's reply to Suchman (entry 10)**: explicit categories make coordination possible. DOI 10.1007/BF00749016. — [record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/27036)

**42. Ananny, Mike & Crawford, Kate (2018). "Seeing without Knowing: Limitations of the Transparency Ideal and Its Application to Algorithmic Accountability." *New Media & Society* 20(3): 973-989. DOI: 10.1177/1461444816676645.** [V] — [record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/ananny-crawford-seeingwithoutknowinglimitationsofthetransparencyidealanditsapplicationtoalgorithmicaccountability-2018)
- Core claim: transparency assumes that seeing a system means knowing and governing it. The authors give ten limits of that ideal (for example, transparency can be disconnected from power, can occlude through volume, and privileges seeing over understanding) and propose accountability that looks across the system rather than inside it **[examples of the ten limits are from memory; the full text is paywalled]**.
- Quote [V-quote, abstract]: "But can 'black boxes' ever be opened, and if so, would that ever be sufficient?"
- Relation to thesis: **The most important challenge to R1.** "A summary you can open back up" must mean more than disclosure: the person must be able to *do* something with what they find. Star & Strauss (entry 8) make the matching point that visibility can also mean surveillance.

- **Hacking's looping (entry 16)** and **Fourcade & Healy (entry 22)** are counter-examples to simple versions of the agency claim and of R2: people use categories on themselves, and fully individual scores can still disempower. — [LRB](https://lrb.co.uk/v28/n16/ian-hacking/making-up-people); [Fourcade & Healy](https://kieranhealy.org/publications/slam/)
- **Christin on Mau (entry 20)**: critiques of metrics tend to assert effects on people in the future tense; a thesis claiming "agency is taken away" should show a mechanism or a case. — [review](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-sociology-archives-europeennes-de-sociologie/article/abs/powerful-metrics-steffen-mau-the-metric-society-on-the-quantification-of-the-social-cambridge-uk-polity-2019-200-p/DD52661A32E870E16AA9F6DF725DBB19)

### Inferences
- Suggested position to defend: *Abstraction is necessary (Simon) and can be empowering (Gigerenzer, Porter). It removes agency when (R1) the reference cannot circulate back (Latour; OECD's own decomposability rule) and the person cannot act on what they find (Ananny & Crawford), or when (R2) a between-person figure is presented as a statement about this person (Molenaar; Sen; Bowker & Star's torque).* Every clause has a cited source above.
- Likely questions and the entry to answer with: "Isn't all data already flattened?" (29-31); "Would you abolish vaccination registers?" (1, 5); "Isn't transparency a naive goal?" (42, 8); "Where is the evidence agency is lost?" (13, 23, and Christin's caution in 20); "Isn't a personal score just as bad?" (22).

### Gaps
- No verified source on public-health risk communication beyond Gigerenzer et al. (for example Spiegelhalter, or Fagerlin and Zikmund-Fisher) was checked.
- O'Neill, Onora, *A Question of Trust* (2002 Reith Lectures) on transparency versus trust, and D'Ignazio & Klein, *Data Feminism* (MIT Press, 2020), are relevant but were **not verified this session**.
- All items marked [memory] or [2nd-quote] need a check against the physical or library copy before being quoted to an academic audience.
